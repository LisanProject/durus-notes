The Author's Opening خُطْبَةُ الْكِتَابِ

Text
الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ، وَأَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَحْدَهُ لَا شَرِيكَ لَهُ، وَأَشْهَدُ أَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا عَبْدُهُ وَرَسُولُهُ، صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَعَلَى آلِهِ وَصَحْبِهِ وَسَلَّمَ.
وَبَعْدُ:

  • لِلَّهِbelongs to Allah
  • رَبِّLord: owner and master, the one who rectifies and nurtures, the one who encompasses and is near
  • الْعَالَمِينَthe worlds, meaning everything besides Allah
  • وَأَشْهَدُand I testify, with certainty in the heart
  • أَنْthat
  • لَاthere is no
  • إِلَهَdeity
  • إِلَّاexcept
  • اللَّهُAllah
  • وَحْدَهُalone
  • شَرِيكَpartner
  • لَهُto Him
  • أَنَّthat (emphatic)
  • مُحَمَّدًاMuhammad, the one praised again and again
  • عَبْدُهُHis servant
  • وَرَسُولُهُand His messenger
  • صَلَّىmay He bless
  • عَلَيْهِupon him
  • وَعَلَىand upon
  • آلِهِhis family or his followers, depending on context
  • وَصَحْبِهِand his companions
  • وَسَلَّمَand grant peace

Translation
All praise belongs to Allah, Lord of all the worlds. I testify that there is no deity except Allah alone, without partner, and I testify that Muhammad is His servant and messenger. May Allah bless him, his family, and his companions, and grant them peace. To proceed.

Why Authors Open This Way

Opening a book with the basmalah, praise of Allah, blessings upon the Prophet, and then "wa ba'd" is a recommended scholarly convention. An author could begin directly with a chapter heading, but the established and better practice is to open in this way. It is also recommended to identify the author and the book at the start.

Sidi Shaykh Abdullah Taha distinguished two separate discussions of the basmalah: its grammatical analysis, which belongs to nahw, and its legal rulings, which belong to fiqh.

Sidi Shaykh Abdullah Taha emphasizes that the opening formula is a recommended convention of the scholars, not an obligatory religious condition for beginning a book.

(1)

Hamd, Rabb, and al-'Alamin

Hamd is verbal commendation intended to honor. Intent and context matter. Words that sound like praise can in fact be mockery or humiliation, as in what is said to a person being punished in the Fire:

ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْعَزِيزُ الْكَرِيمُ
Taste! Indeed, you are the mighty, the noble. (al-Dukhan 44:49)

The words are words of praise, but the meaning is rebuke, so this is not hamd.

Rabb carries several meanings: owner or master, the one who rectifies and nurtures, and the one who encompasses and is near. Al-'alamin is a plural referring to the created worlds, such as the worlds of humans, angels, jinn, and birds, and ultimately everything besides Allah.

A grammatical aside: in the phrase rabb al-'alamin the word appears as al-'alamin because it is in the genitive (majrur) as the second term of an idafa. In the nominative it would be al-'alamun.

Hamd (Praise). Commendation by the tongue, intended to honor and glorify. Words of praise said with the intent of mockery are not hamd.

(2)

The Testimony and Outward Rulings

Ashhadu means more than uttering a phrase. Its essential meaning is certainty. Sidi Shaykh Abdullah Taha then distinguished inward faith from the outward legal judgments people make.

  • A person who believes inwardly but does not pronounce the testimony is not treated as a Muslim in worldly rulings, though, as the Shaykh explained, such a believer is saved in the afterlife by his faith.
  • A person who pronounces the testimony is treated as a Muslim in worldly rulings, even though no one can know whether his heart is sincere.

The worldly consequences include being able to marry a Muslim woman, inheritance, and the protection of one's life. The spoken testimony is therefore the condition for these worldly rulings to apply.

Sidi Shaykh Abdullah Taha emphasizes that people judge by what is outward, statements and actions, while what is inside the heart is known only to Allah.

(3)

The Name Muhammad

Muhammad is a proper name (ism 'alam) taken from a passive participle (ism maf'ul), carrying the meaning of one who is praised again and again. Sidi Shaykh Abdullah Taha contrasted it with the name Muflih, which is taken from an active participle (ism fa'il) of aflaha, to succeed.

He related that the Prophet's grandfather named him Muhammad by divine inspiration. People familiar with the earlier scriptures expected a final prophet named Muhammad, and since Jews and Christians lived in the Arabian Peninsula, some people named their sons Muhammad hoping their child might be that prophet. The Prophet was born in Makkah and migrated to Madinah, a land known for its many date palms.

Ism Maf'ul (Passive Participle). The name Muhammad is related to this form, meaning the one who is praised.

Ism Fa'il (Active Participle). The name Muflih is taken from this form, meaning the one who succeeds.

(4)

Servant Before Messenger

The phrase "His servant and His messenger" places servanthood before messengership because servanthood ('ubudiyyah) is among the greatest attributes of the Prophet.

Who Are the Al?

The meaning of al depends on context. In a general prayer upon the Prophet, it can mean all who follow him. In the chapter of zakah in the Shafi'i school, it has a specific legal meaning: the Prophet's family is Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib, and zakah may not be given to them. The Shaykh also gave illustrative examples where, in another context, "his family" could refer to his followers, or even to the scholars and jurists.

Sidi Shaykh Abdullah Taha emphasizes that the meaning of al in a general prayer is not necessarily its legal meaning in the chapter of zakah. Always ask which context a word appears in before deciding what it refers to.

(5)

Who Is a Companion?

Sahabi (Companion). One who met the Prophet while believing in him and died upon faith.

(6)
  • Abu Jahl met the Prophet but did not believe, so he is not a Companion.
  • One who believed, later apostatized, and died outside Islam does not meet the definition.
  • One who apostatized but returned to Islam and died upon faith remains a Companion.

Sidi Shaykh Abdullah Taha emphasizes the two conditions together: meeting the Prophet as a believer, and dying upon faith. A temporary apostasy followed by a return to Islam does not remove someone from the Companions.

(7)

Wa Ba'd

"Wa ba'd", or "amma ba'd", marks the transition from the introductory remarks to the main subject. Expressions like "hadha" (this) serve a similar purpose of moving from one topic to the next.
