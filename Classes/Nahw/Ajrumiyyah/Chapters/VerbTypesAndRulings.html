The Three Verb Types and Their Base Rulings

Ism and Fi'l, Defined Against Each Other

الِاسْمُ لُغَةً هُوَ مَا يَدُلُّ عَلَى مُسَمًّى، وَاصْطِلَاحاً هُوَ كَلِمَةٌ دَلَّتْ عَلَى مَعْنًى وَلَمْ تَقْتَرِنْ بِزَمَنٍ. الفِعْلُ لُغَةً هُوَ الحَدَثُ. وَفِي اصْطِلَاحِ النُّحَاةِ هُوَ مَا دَلَّ عَلَى مَعْنًى فِي نَفْسِهِ وَاقْتَرَنَ بِزَمَنٍ.
The noun, linguistically, is what indicates a named thing; technically, it is a word indicating a meaning not linked to time. The verb, linguistically, is an event; technically, in the grammarians' terminology, it is what indicates a meaning in itself and is linked to a time.

Definitions of ism and fi'l, as given in this session.

The line drawn between the two is time itself: an ism names something without carrying any tense, a fi'l carries a meaning that is anchored to one of the three times.

The Three Verb Types

Al-Fi'l: a word indicating a meaning in itself, linked to one of the three times, past, present, or imperative.

(1)
ضَرَبَ، يَضْرِبُ، اضْرِبْ.
He hit, he hits, hit!

Worked example of the three verb types.

Al-Madi: An Event Already Past

الفِعْلُ المَاضِي مَا دَلَّ عَلَى حَدَثٍ وَقَعَ قَبْلَ زَمَنِ التَّكَلُّمِ.
The past tense verb is what indicates an event that occurred before the time of speaking.

Definition of al-madi.

جَاءَ، فَالمَجِيءُ هَذَا وَقَعَ قَبْلَ أَنْ أَتَكَلَّمَ بِهِ أَنَا.
"He came," the coming here occurred before I spoke the word myself.

Worked example illustrating al-madi.

Al-Mudari': Present or Expected Future

الفِعْلُ المُضَارِعُ مَا دَلَّ عَلَى حَدَثٍ يَقَعُ فِي الزَّمَانِ الحَاضِرِ أَوْ يُتَوَقَّعُ وُقُوعُهُ فِي الزَّمَنِ المُسْتَقْبَلِ.
The present tense verb is what indicates an event occurring in present time, or expected to occur in future time.

Definition of al-mudari'.

Al-Amr: A Requested Future Event

الأَمْرُ هُوَ لَفْظٌ يُطْلَبُ بِهِ حُصُولُ حَدَثٍ فِي الزَّمَنِ المُسْتَقْبَلِ.
The imperative is an utterance by which the occurrence of an event is requested in future time.

Definition of al-amr.

مَثَلاً إِذَا قُلْتَ لِوَاحِدٍ اجْلِسْ، فَالجُلُوسُ يُطْلَبُ حُصُولُهُ بَعْدَ التَّكَلُّمِ.
For example, if you say to someone "sit," the sitting is requested to occur after the speech itself.

Worked example illustrating al-amr.

The imperative always denotes a future occurrence, never a past or present one.

(1)

Al-Madi Is Built on Fath

اَلْمَاضِي مَفْتُوحُ الآخِرِ أَبَداً.
The past tense verb always has an open (fathah) ending.

The base ruling on al-madi.

ضَرَبَ، جَاءَ، جَلَسَ.
He hit, he came, he sat.

When joined to plural pronouns, the past tense verb is built on dammah instead (e.g. "darabu"). Grammarians differ on how to describe this shift: some hold the fathah is still present in an estimated (muqaddar) sense, others simply call the resulting build dammah outright.

(1)

Ha' al-Ta'nith, Not Ta Marbutah

A terminological aside on the feminine marker itself: the early scholars called it ha' al-ta'nith, not taa marbutah. This second term is a modern designation and does not appear in the early texts, a point that was raised citing Imam al-Shatibi specifically.

Al-Amr Is Built on the Jazm-Sign of Its Mudari'

اَلْأَمْرُ مَبْنِيٌّ عَلَى مَا يُجْزَمُ بِهِ مُضَارِعُهُ.
The imperative is built on whatever sign its present-tense form is made jussive with.

The base ruling on al-amr.

Two worked examples show this in the weak-final-letter case, where the jazm sign is deletion of the weak letter itself, and the imperative simply copies that same deletion: "waqa, yaqi" becomes "qi," and "wa'a, ya'i" becomes "'i."

The Four Markers of the Mudari'

المُضَارِعُ يَبْدَأُ بِإِحْدَى الزَّوَائِدِ الأَرْبَعِ الَّتِي يَجْمَعُهَا قَوْلُكَ: "أَنَيْتَ".
The present tense begins with one of four additional letters, gathered in the mnemonic word "anayta."

The four mudari' markers, gathered in one mnemonic.

Anayta: hamzah (أ), nun (ن), ya' (ي), and ta' (ت), the four letters a mudari' verb must begin with one of. This is a standard memorization tool used across traditional grammar teaching.

(1)

Al-Mudari' Is Raf' by Default

الفِعْلُ المُضَارِعُ مَرْفُوعٌ دَائِماً حَتَّى يَدْخُلَ عَلَيْهِ نَاصِبٌ أَوْ جَازِمٌ.
The present tense verb is always in the raf' case, until a particle of nasb or jazm enters upon it.

The default ruling on al-mudari'.

مُحَمَّدٌ يَأْكُلُ؛ "يَأْكُلُ" فِعْلٌ مُضَارِعٌ مَرْفُوعٌ... لِتَجَرُّدِهِ مِنْ نَاصِبٍ أَوْ جَازِمٍ.
"Muhammad eats"; "ya'kulu" is a present-tense verb in raf', because it is free of any particle of nasb or jazm.

Worked example of default raf' on al-mudari'.

This is exactly the pivot point the rest of the chapter builds from: since the mudari' has a real default state, every remaining section is really about the specific list of particles that knock it out of that default, into nasb or into jazm. (See: The Ten Particles That Cause Nasb, The Eighteen Particles That Cause Jazm)

The Order of the Four Cases in Traditional Teaching

Traditional grammarians list the case markings in a fixed order: raf' (nominative), nasb (accusative/subjunctive), khafd (genitive), then jazm (jussive). This ordering is maintained consistently across teaching.

(1)

Amr, Du'a, and Iltimas: The Same Form, Different Rank

The imperative form itself does not tell you everything about the relationship between speaker and addressee. That is determined by rank.

Al-Amr: issued from someone of higher standing to someone of lower standing.

(1)

Al-Du'a: issued from someone of lower standing to someone of higher standing.

(2)

Al-Iltimas: issued between equals.

(3)

Three examples show all three ranks using the same imperative form: a prophetic command to speak good or remain silent (amr, from the Prophet ﷺ to the believers), the people of the Fire asking the angel to let their Lord decide their fate (a supplication moving upward, though addressed to an angel rather than directly to Allah), and "our Lord, do not take us to account" (du'a, from the believer to Allah). Recognizing which of the three is meant in a given verse or hadith depends entirely on who is actually speaking to whom, not on anything different in the verb's own form.

A Parsing Note: The Ta' of Feminine Address vs. a Case Marker

A student asked whether the kasrah on the ta' in "akalti" (you, feminine, ate) is a case marker or something else entirely.

"Akalti" is a past-tense verb built on sukun, because it is joined to a moving pronoun of raf' (the ta'). The ta' itself marks feminine address; the kasrah on it has nothing to do with khafd or any case inflection at all, since past-tense verbs joined to attached pronouns are built, not declined, in the first place.

(1)

Full parse: "akal," fi'l madi mabni 'ala al-sukun; the ta' is an attached pronoun in the position of raf' as the fa'il (subject); the kasrah on the ta' itself is purely a marker of feminine address, not a case ending.
