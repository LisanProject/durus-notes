The Three Verb Types and Their Base Rulings

Bab al-Af'al بَابُ الْأَفْعَالِ

Text
اَلْأَفْعَالُ ثَلَاثَةٌ: مَاضٍ وَمُضَارِعٌ وَأَمْرٌ، نَحْوَ: ضَرَبَ، وَيَضْرِبُ، وَاضْرِبْ.
Vocabulary
  • اَلْأَفْعَالverbs, the second of the three word categories after ism and harf
  • ثَلَاثَةthree
  • مَاضٍpast tense
  • مُضَارِعpresent tense
  • أَمْرimperative
  • ضَرَبَhe hit (worked example of al-madi)
  • يَضْرِبُhe hits (worked example of al-mudari')
  • اِضْرِبْhit! (worked example of al-amr)
Translation
The chapter of verbs. Verbs are three: past, present, and imperative, such as: he hit, he hits, hit!
Commentary

An ism, linguistically, indicates a named thing, and technically is a word indicating a meaning not linked to time. A fi'l, linguistically, is an event, and technically, in the grammarians' terminology, is what indicates a meaning in itself and is linked to a time. The line drawn between the two categories is time itself: an ism names something without carrying any tense, a fi'l carries a meaning anchored to one of the three times the matn names here.

Al-Madi اَلْمَاضِي

Text
فَالْمَاضِي مَفْتُوحُ الْآخِرِ أَبَدًا.
Vocabulary
  • فَالْمَاضِيso the past tense (verb)
  • مَفْتُوحopened, given a fathah
  • الْآخِرits ending, its final letter
  • أَبَدًاalways, without exception
Translation
The past tense verb always has an open (fathah) ending.

Al-Madi is what indicates an event that occurred before the time of speaking, and is always built on fathah.

(1)
Commentary

جَاءَ, "he came," illustrates the definition itself: the coming occurred before the speaker uttered the word. ضَرَبَ, جَاءَ, جَلَسَ (he hit, he came, he sat) all show the same fathah ending the matn describes.

When joined to plural pronouns, the past tense verb is built on dammah instead, as in ضَرَبُوا. Grammarians differ on how to describe this shift: some hold the fathah is still present in an estimated (muqaddar) sense underneath the dammah, others simply call the resulting build dammah outright.

A terminological aside on the feminine marker seen elsewhere in this science: the early scholars called it ha' al-ta'nith, not taa marbutah. This second term is a modern designation and does not appear in the early texts, a point raised citing Imam al-Shatibi specifically.

Al-Amr اَلْأَمْرُ

Text
وَالْأَمْرُ مَجْزُومٌ أَبَدًا.
Vocabulary
  • وَالْأَمْرand the imperative
  • مَجْزُومin the jazm (jussive) form
  • أَبَدًاalways, without exception
Translation
The imperative is always in the jazm form.

Al-Amr is an utterance by which the occurrence of an event is requested in future time, and is always built on the jazm form.

(1)
Commentary

اِجْلِسْ, "sit," illustrates the definition: the sitting is requested to occur only after the speech itself, never in the past or present.

Two worked examples show what "majzum" looks like in the weak-final-letter case, where the jazm sign is deletion of the weak letter itself, and the imperative simply carries that same deletion over: وَقَى، يَقِي becomes قِ, and وَعَى، يَعِي becomes عِ.

Amr, du'a, and iltimas share this exact grammatical form while differing entirely in speaker rank: amr moves from higher to lower, du'a from lower to higher, and iltimas between equals. A prophetic command to speak good or remain silent illustrates amr, the people of the Fire asking an angel to let their Lord decide their fate illustrates a supplication moving upward, and "our Lord, do not take us to account" illustrates du'a addressed directly to Allah. Recognizing which of the three is meant depends entirely on who is speaking to whom, never on anything different in the verb's own form.

Al-Mudari' اَلْمُضَارِعُ

Text
وَالمُضَارِعُ مَا كَانَ فِي أَوَّلِهِ إِحْدَى الزَّوَائِدِ الأَرْبَعِ الَّتِي يَجْمَعُهَا قَوْلُكَ: (أَنَيْتُ)، وَهُوَ مَرْفُوعٌ أَبَدًا حَتَّى يَدْخُلَ عَلَيْهِ نَاصِبٌ أَوْ جَازِمٌ.
Vocabulary
  • وَالمُضَارِعand the present tense verb
  • أَوَّلِهits beginning
  • الزَّوَائِد الأَرْبَعthe four additional letters
  • أَنَيْتُ"anaytu," the mnemonic word gathering the four markers: hamzah, nun, ya', ta'
  • مَرْفُوعin the raf' (nominative) case
  • نَاصِبa particle causing nasb
  • جَازِمa particle causing jazm
Translation
The present tense verb is that which has, at its start, one of the four additional letters gathered in your saying "anaytu," and it is always in raf' until a particle of nasb or jazm enters upon it.

Al-Mudari' indicates an event occurring now or expected in the future, is marked by one of the four anaytu letters, and defaults to raf' until a nasib or jazim particle enters.

(1)
Commentary

مُحَمَّدٌ يَأْكُلُ, "Muhammad eats," illustrates the default case directly: يَأْكُلُ is a present-tense verb in raf' because it is free of any particle of nasb or jazm.

This default raf' is the pivot the rest of the chapter builds from. Every remaining section is really about the specific list of particles that knock the mudari' out of this default, into nasb or into jazm. (See: The Ten Particles That Cause Nasb, The Eighteen Particles That Cause Jazm)

Traditional grammarians list the case markings in a fixed order: raf', nasb, khafd, then jazm, maintained consistently across teaching.

A Parsing Note: The Ta' of Feminine Address

Commentary

I asked whether the kasrah on the ta' in أَكَلْتِ (you, feminine, ate) is a case marker or something else entirely. Full parse: أَكَلْ is fi'l madi mabni 'ala al-sukun; the ta' is an attached pronoun in the position of raf' as the fa'il (subject); the kasrah on the ta' itself is purely a marker of feminine address, not a case ending, since past tense verbs joined to attached pronouns are built (mabni), not declined, in the first place.
