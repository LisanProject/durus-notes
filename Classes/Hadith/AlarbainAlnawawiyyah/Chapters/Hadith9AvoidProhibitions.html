Hadith 9: Avoid What I Forbid, Do What You Can of What I Command

Text

عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ عَبْدِ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنِ صَخْرٍ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: سَمِعْتُ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ يَقُولُ: «مَا نَهَيْتُكُمْ عَنْهُ فَاجْتَنِبُوهُ، وَمَا أَمَرْتُكُمْ بِهِ فَأْتُوا مِنْهُ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُمْ، فَإِنَّمَا أَهْلَكَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ كَثْرَةُ مَسَائِلِهِمْ وَاخْتِلَافُهُمْ عَلَى أَنْبِيَائِهِمْ».
On the authority of Abu Hurayrah, 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Sakhr, may Allah be pleased with him, who said: I heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ say: "Whatever I have forbidden you, avoid it. Whatever I have commanded you, do of it what you are able. What destroyed those before you was only their excessive questioning and their opposing their prophets."

Narrated by Abu Hurayrah, and collected by al-Bukhari and Muslim with slightly different wording. Hadith nine of Imam al-Nawawi's collection.

Homework The Shaykh asked each student to write down the exact Arabic wording of the narration they are using and determine whether it is al-Bukhari's or Muslim's. This was left open in class.

Translating It Together

Several students offered translations. The class agreed that "excessive questioning" captures kathratu masa'ilihim, and the Shaykh accepted both "differing with" and "contradicting" for ikhtilafuhum 'ala anbiya'ihim. He asked each student to prepare a translation in advance so the class can compare versions and curate a shared one. He accepted standard transliteration systems such as the Library of Congress system, as long as one is used consistently, and recommended keeping Arabic technical terms when English would lose their specialized meaning.

"Avoid It": The Force of the Prohibition

"Whatever I have forbidden you, avoid it" is a categorical command, and a translation should keep that force. Avoiding (ijtinab) means more than stopping at the last moment. It means not approaching the act, its causes, or the routes that lead to it, and keeping away from what makes it appealing. Detailed legal discussion does identify exceptions, but those are established by the Sharia itself.

Ijtinab (Avoidance). Keeping a distance from the prohibited act and from what leads to it, not merely refraining once one is already close.

(1)

A student asked whether this applies differently to major and minor sins. The Shaykh answered that the basic command against intentionally and willingly committing a prohibited act covers both, though sins differ in weight. Repeating or justifying minor sins can amount to fisq, as can committing a major sin. This is distinct from necessity or coercion, and sincere repentance is accepted by Allah.

"Do of It What You Are Able"

The command, by contrast, is tied to human capacity. Do the whole act when you can; if the whole is impossible, do the parts that remain within your ability. Being unable to complete the act does not excuse every part of it.

Examples. Pray sitting if unable to stand, and lying down if unable to sit. In wudu, wash or wipe whatever portion of the limb one is able to.

(1)

The Shaykh cautioned that this does not license inventing partial forms of worship or transactions. A student must learn which elements of an act are essential, which are obligatory but not essential, and which are recommended. In prayer, the arkan are essential; sujud al-sahw covers certain non-essential omissions, obligatory acts and, in some opinions, recommended ones. Sitting may replace standing because the Sharia established that substitution, not because someone devised it.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that reduced or substitute forms of an act must be established by the Sharia, not invented by individuals.

(2)

Capacity Is Not Convenience

Istita'ah means ability, not ease. People may try to excuse themselves, but convenience is not a legal criterion unless the Sharia makes it one. Waking for Fajr, or staying awake for 'Isha in some places and seasons, is hard; jihad is not convenient. A mujtahid sincerely seeks Allah's ruling, not the most convenient interpretation, and convenience, which varies by time and place, should not be slipped in as a qualification.

Genuine excuses recognized by the ShariaNot excuses in themselves
Physical inabilityReluctance
Absence of meansInconvenience or difficulty
CoercionLack of enthusiasm or preference
NecessityA self-created hardship

Jumu'ah is obligatory on a resident adult Muslim man, yet coercive conditions that prevent attendance may be a genuine excuse, as may needing to stay home to protect one's family. A person in the desert with nothing to sustain life except something ordinarily prohibited is another case: the thing remains prohibited in itself, but his situation affects his legal and moral responsibility. These are exceptions the Sharia recognizes, not a general permission to make exceptions.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that capacity is not convenience. Legally relevant inability, and every exception, must be grounded in the Sharia.

(1)

Why Avoiding Harm Comes First

The hadith treats the two halves differently: the prohibition is unqualified, while the command is tied to ability. The Shaykh connected this to the legal maxim:

دَرْءُ المَفَاسِدِ مُقَدَّمٌ عَلَى جَلْبِ المَصَالِحِ
دَرْءُ المَفَاسِدِ أَوْلَى مِنْ جَلْبِ المَصَالِحِ
Warding off harms takes precedence over bringing benefits. Warding off harms is more deserving than bringing benefits.

Avoiding prohibited harm is not conditioned on capacity in the way a commanded good is, and its exceptions are found in the Sharia. For the soul, avoiding the prohibited matters more than adding acts of worship while continuing to indulge in what is prohibited. The Shaykh also alluded to a hadith promising Paradise to one who fulfills the obligations and refrains from the prohibitions, without giving its wording in class.

Falling Short, and Returning

People will still fall into sin. The commitment is to orient one's intention toward avoiding the prohibited, and to renew it through tawbah and istighfar with a resolve not to return. The Shaykh distinguished an unwanted temptation or inclination, which lies outside one's will, from a deliberate decision to cultivate or pursue it. His example was a person inclined toward same-sex relations who struggles not to commit the prohibited act: he is rewarded for striving against himself, and the inclination does not change the prohibition.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that an inclination outside one's will is not the same as deliberately seeking it, and that the door of repentance stays open.

(1)

"What Destroyed Those Before You"

The hadith names two causes of the destruction of earlier peoples: excessive questioning and opposing their prophets. Asking is not blameworthy in itself. A question can be recommended, or even obligatory when it is the only way to learn what one must do. Questions that clarify a command or prohibition, or prepare for a situation one can realistically expect, are beneficial. The problem is excess: questions asked to argue, to burden, or to get answers that will not benefit the asker, like a child repeating "Why?" to annoy rather than to learn.

Beneficial questionsBlameworthy questions
Learning what one is obligated to doHypotheticals with no real need
Clarifying a command or prohibitionRepeated probing after an adequate answer
Preparing for a realistic situationDemanding further distinctions after sufficient guidance
Sincere inquiry for understanding and actionTesting or trying to trick a scholar or prophet
Asking for status, or to show cleverness in disputes
Challenging the Sharia or its qualified transmitters with no aim of understanding

Salman asking the Prophet ﷺ about the signs of prophethood was genuine inquiry, not a trick. The balance is to ask sincerely, for understanding and for action, not to ask assumptive, theoretical, performative, or oppositional questions.

Ikhtilafuhum, Not Ikhtilafihim

The Shaykh drew attention to the case ending. The verb ikhtalafa 'ala fulan can mean going back and forth to someone, visiting him repeatedly. But the hadith reads:

وَاخْتِلَافُهُمْ عَلَى أَنْبِيَائِهِمْ

in the nominative, joined to kathratu as a second thing that destroyed them, not in the genitive as if it were another of their "questions." Here 'ala means "against," not "visiting," and the word means opposition and disagreement: giving their prophets a hard time.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that the grammatical form and case ending change the meaning here, from repeated visiting to opposing the prophets.

(1)

Benefits

  • Prohibitions are avoided categorically, along with their causes and routes.
  • A command is performed in full when possible, and in part when only part is possible.
  • Capacity is not convenience; inability and exceptions must be grounded in the Sharia.
  • Partial or substitute forms of worship must be ones the Sharia established.
  • Warding off harm takes precedence over bringing benefit.
  • Questions can be good or even necessary; excess, argument, and display are what is blamed.
  • An unwanted inclination is not the same as deliberate sin, and repentance remains open.
