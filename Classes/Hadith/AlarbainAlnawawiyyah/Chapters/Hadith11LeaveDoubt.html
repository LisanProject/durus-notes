Hadith 11: Leave What Causes You Doubt

Text

عَنْ أَبِي مُحَمَّدٍ الحَسَنِ بْنِ عَلِيِّ بْنِ أَبِي طَالِبٍ، سِبْطِ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ وَرَيْحَانَتِهِ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُمَا، قَالَ: حَفِظْتُ مِنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ: «دَعْ مَا يَرِيبُكَ إِلَى مَا لَا يَرِيبُكَ». رَوَاهُ التِّرْمِذِيُّ وَالنَّسَائِيُّ، وَقَالَ التِّرْمِذِيُّ: حَدِيثٌ حَسَنٌ صَحِيحٌ.
On the authority of Abu Muhammad al-Hasan ibn 'Ali ibn Abi Talib, the grandson of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and his beloved, may Allah be pleased with them both, who said: I memorized from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ: "Leave what causes you doubt for what does not cause you doubt." Narrated by al-Tirmidhi and al-Nasa'i; al-Tirmidhi said: a hasan sahih hadith.
Vocabulary
  • سِبْطِgrandson; the prophetic term for his grandsons, here through Fatimah
  • وَرَيْحَانَتِهِand his rayhanah: his sweet-smelling flower, a term of affection
  • حَفِظْتُI memorized; stresses direct attention and retention
  • دَعْleave, abandon; a practical instruction to move on
  • مَا يَرِيبُكَwhat causes you rayb, genuine doubt and the unease that comes with it
  • إِلَى مَا لَا يَرِيبُكَfor what does not cause you doubt, what is clear

Source and Grading

Collected by al-Tirmidhi and al-Nasa'i, and graded by al-Tirmidhi "hasan sahih," the very expression the class had just studied (see "Hasan Sahih" and al-Tirmidhi's Definition of Hasan). Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin said this wording is part of a longer narration from al-Hasan that includes the qunut of witr, narrated by Ahmad, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i, Ibn Hibban, and al-Hakim, and he regarded al-Tirmidhi and al-Nasa'i as the highest of those collections. No detailed analysis of the chain was given.

From Doubt Toward Clarity

The central lesson is to move away from genuine religious uncertainty toward clarity when a clear alternative is available. This is wara', scrupulousness. The hadith pairs with Hadith 6: that one is more conceptual and cautionary, describing halal, haram, and the doubtful and warning against grazing near the boundaries; this one is directly practical, telling you to step away from genuine doubt.

It is not an instruction to ruminate, or to treat every feeling of unease as proof that something is haram. The command is practical: recognize a genuine doubt as defined through the Quran and Sunnah, leave the matter when there is a clear alternative, and move on. Rayb is inward unease tied to real uncertainty, not any passing worry. Purely halal matters should not produce it. If something permissible takes time from a greater priority, one may leave it for that reason, but that is not leaving it because it is doubtful or haram.

Wara'. Guarding oneself against questionable matters out of concern that they may lead to the prohibited. It may lead a spiritually disciplined person to leave something legally permissible when there is a genuine concern it could lead him into the haram. This is an additional station of taqwa, not the basic legal duty shared by every Muslim.

Three Cases That Are Not This Hadith

The Shaykh posed three examples and asked which applies the hadith. It was a trick question: none of them is the optional, ethical case the hadith describes.

Case 1Food you know contains something prohibited. Is leaving it an application of "leave what causes you doubt"?

No. The prohibition is known, not doubtful, so leaving it is a legal obligation, not the optional space of wara'.

Case 2You are certain you made wudu and have no evidence it was broken, but later an unsupported doubt arises. Should you leave your wudu "because of doubt"?

No. The established certainty remains, by istishab al-asl, presuming that an established state continues. The Shaykh cited Ibn Rajab in Jami' al-'Ulum wa al-Hikam against using this hadith to establish waswasa. The maxim:

اليَقِينُ لَا يُتْرَكُ لِمُجَرَّدِ الشَّكِّ

Certainty is not abandoned for mere doubt.

Case 3You feel anxious about something established as halal. Does the anxiety make it doubtful, so you should leave it?

No. Anxiety does not make a halal matter prohibited. Learn the ruling rather than inferring it from nervousness. Misreading the hadith can feed anxiety and obsessive avoidance, and psychological distress may need treatment rather than being given the force of a religious ruling.

Three Categories, Three Responses

CategoryResponse
Established haramMust be avoided: a legal obligation.
Genuinely doubtful (shubhah)May be left as wara' when a clear alternative exists. This is the hadith.
Established halalRemains permissible; unsupported unease does not change the ruling.

Waswasa. Intrusive, unsupported whispering or obsessive doubt. It is not wara', and discomfort alone does not mean one must stop.

(1)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that this hadith concerns genuine doubt, not known prohibition and not unsupported anxiety. Do not use it to create waswasa or to forbid yourself what is established as halal.

(2)

Wara' Is Not Always the Strictest Opinion

Wara' does not mean choosing the strictest view on every issue. The Shaykh recalled the three young Companions who resolved to exceed the Prophet's ﷺ practice in fasting, marriage, and night prayer, and the Prophet ﷺ forbade their excess. The religion includes rukhas, concessions, and Allah loves that His concessions be taken just as His ordinary rulings are observed.

Nor does the hadith license following one's feelings in everything. The Shaykh warned against applying the hadith about consulting one's heart indiscriminately: it was addressed to a guided, educated Companion whose heart was formed by the Quran and Sunnah, not a license to treat every feeling as a ruling from Allah. A sound heart remains subordinate to revelation and established legal judgments.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that the Prophet's ﷺ guidance includes accepting lawful concessions and forbids excess, and that the heart's feelings remain subordinate to revelation.

(1)

Benefits

  • A believer may leave a genuinely doubtful matter for a clear alternative as an act of wara'.
  • Known haram is left because it is prohibited, not as optional caution.
  • Unsupported doubt does not overturn certainty, including certainty of having wudu.
  • Anxiety about something established as halal does not make it prohibited.
  • Wara' is not a mandate to take the strictest opinion; lawful concessions are part of the Sunnah.
  • Learning the ruling is better than deriving a prohibition from unease.
  • This concise prophetic maxim is worth memorizing, as al-Hasan did.

Follow-Up: When a Text Seems Clear

The next class opened with a question growing out of this hadith. What about someone who treats a hadith on trimming the beard or on garments below the ankles as self-evidently decisive, dismisses the explanations of scholars and reports from the Companions, and concludes that anyone acting differently is committing haram? And in disputed financial matters, such as buying stocks or an Islamic-finance loan, if one person follows a recognized school's rules and regards something as halal while another avoids it as doubtful, can clarity differ from person to person?

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin answered that a layperson must not treat a text as self-interpreting, or cut it off from its context, the other evidence, and the methods of usul al-fiqh. An apparent command or prohibition creates an immediate duty: pause and learn what it requires. Someone qualified may reason through the evidence; someone who is not must ask the people of knowledge. A translation or surface reading that seems clear does not tell a layperson the scope, limits, conditions, and pillars of the ruling.

Example: "Establish the prayer and give zakah." The command is clear and must be acted on, yet establishing prayer means more than doing something called prayer, and fulfilling it requires learning its details. That practice has been taught across generations by scholars, imams, teachers, and families.

(1)

People understand texts according to their levels, and it is often a scholar's teaching that makes a ruling clear to an ordinary Muslim. A layperson can mistake following a particular scholar for weighing the evidence himself. On finance, the Shaykh cited the late Shaykh Ahmad Taha Rayan, who studied law, economics, finance, and Sharia, and held that riba is forbidden and the contemporary banking system is built on it, while noting that other learned scholars reach different conclusions about banking transactions. He warned that acting on isolated readings without qualified understanding can do grave harm, giving the example of sincere people who joined ISIS believing they were following clear texts.

He again cautioned against presenting "ask your heart" as guidance for everyone. It was addressed to a particular Companion the Prophet ﷺ recognized as knowledgeable, sincere, and sound in judgment. Some advanced students may be permitted by their teachers to weigh opinions for their own practice without being qualified to give fatwa to others. Telling unqualified people to follow their hearts, especially when they already want a certain outcome, increases confusion.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes humility: when a text seems clear, pause, learn, and ask the people of knowledge rather than acting on a surface reading.

(2)

From Class: "Said" or "Says"?

The Shaykh raised whether to say the Prophet ﷺ and the scholars "said" or "say" something. For the Prophet ﷺ he strongly preferred the present tense, out of adab, because his teachings remain in force, and for metaphysical reasons he holds. For the scholars he had not settled on a rule. The students favored the present tense, one noting that it shapes a listener's first impression and another that beneficial knowledge keeps benefiting its scholars, while one cautioned that tense alone will not change the minds of those starting from a different paradigm. The Shaykh left the question open. He added that belief in a particular karamah, or a particular scholar's spiritual standing, should not be imposed on others, while denying karamat altogether is contrary to the Sunni position.
