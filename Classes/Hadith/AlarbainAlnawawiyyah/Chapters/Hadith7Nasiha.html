Hadith 7: The Religion Is Sincere Counsel

Text

عَنْ أَبِي رُقَيَّةَ تَمِيمِ بْنِ أَوْسٍ الدَّارِيِّ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ، أَنَّ النَّبِيَّ ﷺ قَالَ: "الدِّينُ النَّصِيحَةُ". قُلْنَا: لِمَنْ؟ قَالَ: "لِلَّهِ، وَلِكِتَابِهِ، وَلِرَسُولِهِ، وَلِأَئِمَّةِ المُسْلِمِينَ، وَعَامَّتِهِمْ."
On the authority of Abu Ruqayyah Tamim ibn Aws al-Dari, that the Prophet ﷺ said: "The religion is sincere counsel." We said: "To whom?" He said: "To Allah, to His Book, to His Messenger, and to the leaders of the Muslims and their common folk."

Narrated by Muslim.

"The Religion Is Sincere Counsel": A Statement of Restriction

The hadith uses a rhetorical device of restriction (qasr) in its very structure: "al-dinu al-nasihah," the religion is [nothing but] sincere counsel. Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin compared this to the well-known phrasing "al-hajju 'Arafah," Hajj is [standing at] 'Arafah, which does not mean Hajj literally consists of nothing else, but that this single element is so central that the rest of the ritual is built around it. In the same way, nasiha is not merely one virtue among many in the religion. It is being presented as the axis the whole of din turns on.

The Five Objects of Nasiha

The Prophet ﷺ answers "to whom" with five objects, and each carries a distinct meaning once unpacked rather than left as a single undifferentiated idea of "being sincere."

Nasiha to Allah: fulfilling the rights owed to Him, directing pure, sincere belief toward Him alone, and striving to obey His commands.

(1)

Nasiha to His Book: believing in its divine origin, honoring the mushaf physically by placing it above other books, and learning and acting upon its judgments rather than treating it only as an object of reverence.

(2)

Nasiha to His Messenger: affirming his message, following his example, and loving his family and his Companions.

(3)

Nasiha to the Leaders of the Muslims: assisting legitimate rulers, obeying them in what is true, and alerting them to their mistakes gently and privately rather than publicly.

(4)

Nasiha to the Common Muslims: guiding them toward what benefits them, and covering their private faults (satr al-'awrah) rather than exposing them.

(5)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin gave concrete practical detail on the second object specifically: physically placing the Qur'an in the most honorable position available, reciting it with proper tajwid, and actively seeking to apply its message rather than treating recitation as an end in itself. He also noted, as an aside prompted by the narrator's name, that taking a kunyah (a name like "Abu Ruqayyah") from one's daughter, not only a son, is an honored and fully permissible practice, as this hadith's own narrator illustrates.

Correcting Leaders Privately, and Covering Faults Among the Community

The counsel owed to leaders and the counsel owed to ordinary people are not the same in kind. Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin drew out the difference in rights between public and private figures when it comes to criticism: a ruler's mistake is corrected quietly and directly, not aired publicly, while an ordinary person's private fault is something the community is meant to cover rather than expose.

He stressed that the entire purpose of nasiha is always to bring about good, never harm, and recounted how the imams of the past would try to cover one another's mistakes rather than publicize them, unless an ongoing fitnah made public clarification unavoidable. He gave the example of scholarly discussions surrounding the last Ottoman Khalifah as a case where this tension between private counsel and public necessity has come up in practice.

Fard Kifayah: a communal obligation, one that is lifted from the community as a whole once it has been fulfilled by some of its members.

(1)

Giving nasiha is generally a fard kifayah rather than an individual absolute duty resting on every single Muslim at all times.

Nasiha in the Household, and Whether It Falls on the Layperson

Beyond the communal level, there is a personal obligation to advise those in one's immediate circle about basic religious duties, such as prayer. Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin stressed hikmah (wisdom) and diplomacy specifically in family settings, explaining that advice should sometimes be deferred if giving it risks causing more harm than benefit. He noted that both spouses owe this counsel to each other, not just one direction, and warned that passivity among the 'ulama specifically allows ignorance and misguidance to spread through a community.

A student asked directly whether an ordinary layperson is obligated to give nasiha, using the example of seeing one's own brother neglecting prayer.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin's answer referenced Imam Ibn Qudamah: nasiha is a communal obligation (fard kifayah), and once some members of the community carry it out, the rest are not held accountable for failing to. He laid out the structure in tiers: the ruler changes wrong by the hand, scholars advise by speech, and ordinary believers who are unable to do either act with the heart instead. Within one's own family and close circle specifically, advice should still be given, but with hikmah, and deferred where real harm is likely, always intending good for the person being advised.

(1)

Closing: Purifying One's Own Heart First

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin closed the session's treatment of this hadith by stressing that the entire point of nasiha is the salvation and benefit of the person receiving it, which requires the one giving counsel to purify his own heart first, rather than treating advice as an occasion for self-righteousness or correction for its own sake.
