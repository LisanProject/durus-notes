Hadith 8: Commanded to Fight Until They Testify

Text

أُمِرْتُ أَنْ أُقَاتِلَ النَّاسَ حَتَّى يَشْهَدُوا أَنْ لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَأَنَّ مُحَمَّداً رَسُولُ اللَّهِ، وَيُقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَيُؤْتُوا الزَّكَاةَ، فَإِذَا فَعَلُوا ذَلِكَ عَصَمُوا مِنِّي دِمَاءَهُمْ وَأَمْوَالَهُمْ إِلَّا بِحَقِّ الإِسْلَامِ، وَحِسَابُهُمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ تَعَالَى.
I have been commanded to fight the people until they testify that there is no deity but Allah and that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah, establish the prayer, and give zakat. If they do so, they have protected from me their blood and wealth except by the right of Islam, and their reckoning is with Allah the Exalted.

Narrated by 'Abdullah ibn 'Umar, and collected by al-Bukhari and Muslim. Hadith eight of Imam al-Nawawi's collection.

"I Have Been Commanded": A Passive Verb Carrying Divine Weight

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasized the significance of the passive construction "umirtu" (I have been commanded), rather than an active phrasing naming a human authority. The passive voice signals that the instruction originates from Allah Himself, adding a gravity and reverence to the command that a merely human directive would not carry, and placing sharp limits on who can invoke it, since it is not a general license anyone can claim for themselves.

The passive also magnifies the unnamed Commander and the weight of the command, and presents the instruction as divine rather than personal. "An uqatila" prepares the listener for fighting, but its object and scope can only be understood through the conditions and qualifications that follow.

Who "The People" Refers To

"An-nas" (the people) in this hadith is not a reference to humanity at large or to the People of the Book specifically.

An-Nas: understood by the dominant view among the classical 'ulama to refer specifically to the Arab idolaters (mushrikin) of the Prophet's ﷺ own time, not to all people in general and not to the People of the Book.

(1)

Some scholars took it to mean the People of the Book. The dominant reading rests on the context in which the Prophet spoke, and on other places where "an-nas" names a specific group rather than all people. Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin stressed that this is the traditional scholarly reading, not a modern attempt to soften the hadith.

Until They Testify, Pray, and Give Zakah

"Until" (hatta) marks an endpoint. Fighting is not an end in itself; the command is limited by the goal it names.

"They testify" (yashhadu) means genuine acknowledgment and open declaration, not just saying the words. The two testimonies are inseparable. Where a narration mentions only "la ilaha illa Allah", other narrations add belief in the Prophet and what he brought, and the full testimony is meant. A Muslim needs firm, certain belief, but mastering formal theology is not a condition of Islam. The Shaykh gave the familiar example of the Bedouin: tracks in the desert point to the sheep that passed, and the traces of creation point to the Creator. The Prophet accepted firm assent without demanding technical proofs.

"They establish the prayer" means performing it as the Prophet taught and keeping it up, not merely acknowledging that it is obligatory. Prayer is also a communal practice that holds the Ummah together. That the imam must act if a whole community abandons prayer is agreed upon across the schools; whether he must intervene in each individual's case is disputed.

"They give zakah": zakah is a right attached to wealth, ordinarily paid to the legitimate ruler or delivered to those entitled to it.

"So when they do that" (fa-idha): idha carries a sense of expectation and hope. The hadith opens with the heavy "I have been commanded to fight" and moves toward the hoped-for outcome: the conditions met and protection in force. That protection is broader than being spared from battle. It secures their lives and property against any threat, "blood" standing for the whole person, and property named explicitly.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes the difference between entering Islam and the obligations that follow. The testimony establishes a person's Muslim status and protection; it does not make prayer, zakah, or any other obligation irrelevant.

(1)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that firm, certain belief is what is required of a Muslim, not mastery of formal theology.

Judging by Outward Action, Not Inward Sincerity

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin connected this scope to the broader authority and reach of the command: the hadith establishes that judgment is made by outward actions alone, the testimony, the prayer, the zakat, while sincerity of the heart is known only to Allah. He repeatedly and firmly cautioned that this hadith does not license individuals or groups to investigate people's private religious status or act as informal religious police. Only the Prophet ﷺ himself, and after him a legitimate state or imam, holds the authority this hadith describes, and even a legitimate state's authority to police religious matters has real limits, it is not unbounded simply because it is institutional.

"And their reckoning is with Allah" settles where final judgment lies. The Prophet was given knowledge of some people's hearts by Allah, such as the hypocrites, whose names he shared with Hudhayfah, yet their outward submission still protected their lives and property in this world. If that held for the Prophet, it holds all the more for later rulers, who inherited no such knowledge. The Shaykh also cited the hadith of the two litigants: the more eloquent one may win the outward judgment even when the truth is against him, and the verdict does not change the hidden reality, which Allah will judge.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes: people judge outward conduct and evidence. Hidden sincerity, hypocrisy, and disbelief belong to Allah's knowledge alone.

(1)

Abu Bakr and 'Umar's Disagreement Over the Zakat Refusers

The class examined the well-known disagreement during the Ridda wars over fighting those who refused to pay zakat while still openly declaring the Shahadah.

Abu Bakr's Position: refusal to pay zakat constituted organized resistance to an obligation attached to Islam itself, and this warranted military intervention by the state.

(1)

'Umar's Initial Position: reciting the Shahadah alone protected life and wealth, so withholding zakat by itself did not justify the use of force.

(2)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin recounted the back-and-forth between the two caliphs and highlighted that Abu Bakr's position, endorsing state intervention against organized, public refusal, became the settled scholarly consensus, while 'Umar's initial approach considered only the first "circle," the bare testimony, as sufficient for protection. This episode established the principle that the Prophet's ﷺ and the caliph's institutional authority extends to enforcing communal obligations like zakat when a group collectively resists them, a precedent for public governance specifically, and, he warned, not a model that licenses individual vigilantism in any form.

A student asked whether the disagreement between the Sahabah was actually about whether "the people" in the hadith includes Muslims who refuse zakat.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin clarified that the disagreement was not about the scope of "an-nas" at all. It was about "bihaqqiha," by its due right, the rights and obligations bundled together with the Shahadah. 'Umar treated the testimony itself as the primary, sufficient protection, while Abu Bakr held that the obligations bundled in with it, zakat among them, and its rightful submission to the state, were included as part of what the Shahadah's protection actually required.

(3)

The Shaykh presented Abu Bakr's reading of "except by its right" as the stronger and more comprehensive one: the rights attached to the testimony include the obligations of Islam, and he would not separate zakah from prayer. 'Umar accepted his reasoning. The case concerned a legitimate ruler facing organized, public refusal by groups with shawkah, and the Shaykh described the majority, which he would call a consensus, as allowing the legitimate ruler to address such groups.

Shawkah: strength or armed power. The public case in question is organized resistance to public obligations by a group with shawkah, against legitimate authority.

(4)

Asked why fasting and Hajj are not named alongside prayer and zakah, students suggested that Hajj depends on ability, and that fasting has exceptions and is an intimate act between the servant and Allah. The Shaykh answered that all the other obligations are included in the wider meaning of the testimony and what the Prophet brought. If an organized part of the community collectively abandoned an outward obligation, the legitimate imam's duty would be to facilitate the establishment of the religion, including addressing public wrongs such as dishonesty in the markets. None of this authorizes policing private failings or hidden beliefs.

Two Kinds of Jihad, and Who May Declare Either

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin extensively clarified that the command "umirtu" applies to the Prophet ﷺ and to legitimate rulers after him, never to individuals acting on their own authority. He distinguished between two categories of jihad relevant to understanding this hadith's limits.

Jihad al-Difa' (Defensive Jihad): requires no prior formal state declaration when a community is already under attack.

(1)

Jihad al-Talab (Offensive Jihad): requires a formal call issued by the state or a legitimate imam before it can be undertaken at all.

(2)

Institutional, legitimate authority is a precondition for the use of force in matters of faith and communal obligation in either case. He fervently warned students never to confuse scholarly discussion, institutional authority, or legitimate public governance with private enforcement or zealotry carried out by individuals or informal groups.

He closed the discussion with a prayer that the Ummah be granted sound institutional leadership.
