Hadith 12: Leaving What Does Not Concern You

Text

عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: قَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: «مِنْ حُسْنِ إِسْلَامِ المَرْءِ تَرْكُهُ مَا لَا يَعْنِيهِ». حَدِيثٌ حَسَنٌ، رَوَاهُ التِّرْمِذِيُّ وَغَيْرُهُ هَكَذَا.
On the authority of Abu Hurayrah, may Allah be pleased with him, who said: the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "An important part of the excellence of a person's Islam is his leaving what does not concern him." A hasan hadith, narrated by al-Tirmidhi and others in this way.
Vocabulary
  • مِنْpart of; here partitive, not "from" in the sense of origin
  • حُسْنِthe excellence, beauty, completeness of
  • إِسْلَامِ المَرْءِa person's Islam
  • تَرْكُهُhis leaving, abandoning
  • مَا لَا يَعْنِيهِwhat does not concern him
Homework Investigate this hadith's isnad and grading, and identify the main scholarly views on it. No final grading or detailed isnad analysis was given in class.

Husn and Ihsan

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin preferred conveying min as "an important part of," since it marks a portion, not an origin. Asked how husn relates to ihsan, he said they share a root: husn is a state of beauty, excellence, or completeness, while ihsan is the ongoing process of perfecting and beautifying. This beauty is essential, not decorative. It includes the beauty of the heart, of speech, of attitude, and of wishing and sharing good for others, as well as outward beauty. He noted how hard these words are to carry into English.

'Anahu al-amr. A matter "concerned" him: it became an object of his attention and care ('inayah). What does not concern him is what does not deserve that attention.

(1)

Not "Mind Your Own Business"

The hadith should not be reduced to the modern slogan "mind your own business." What concerns a person is set by the Sharia, not by personal interest, desire (hawa), or contemporary assumptions. The Shaykh cited Ibn Rajab: the measure is the judgment of the Sharia and of Islam, not one's inclinations. What Islam requires of a person remains his concern even when it involves other people.

Still your concernNot your concern
Caring for family, fulfilling trustsOthers' private conversations and messages
Helping strangers and people in needMarital secrets and disagreements
Sincere advice, when appropriate and under its conditionsOther people's mistakes, intentions, and family problems
Teaching what you have masteredTheir personal decisions
Seeking true and necessary knowledge, and asking needed questionsRumor, and curiosity about celebrities' relationships

The opposite error is to assume everything concerns a Muslim, an appetite for unnecessary involvement that feeds rumor culture. The Shaykh referred to Ibn Hajar's examples of distractions: unnecessary speech (fudul al-kalam), unnecessary permissible activities, seeking reputation or position, and excess that brings no final benefit.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that the standard is not hawa but the judgment of the Sharia. Learn what the Sharia defines as your business before applying the hadith.

(1)

What Does Concern You

A person's legitimate concerns fall broadly into what preserves this life and what secures the next (fi al-hal wa al-ma'al). Worldly necessities include basic needs and learning a lawful, dignified craft or trade. The hereafter includes Islam, iman, and ihsan: fulfilling obligations, leaving prohibitions, and attending to the disliked, doubtful, and unnecessary when they hold back spiritual progress. The point is not to abandon everything like a monk, but to redirect attention.

Concerns youDoes not concern you
GoodDo itLeave it
BadLeave itLeave it

In explaining this matrix, the Shaykh distinguished actively doing what is good and relevant from actively refraining from what is bad, and both from simply passing over an irrelevant matter without making a project of it. The result: engage positively with what is good and genuinely yours, and leave the rest.

Two commands in one. Tark al-fudul, leaving excess, and al-qiyam bi al-muhimm, carrying out what matters. A student who stops wasting time but still neglects prayer, study, family duties, and assigned work has done only half. The Shaykh cited Ibn Daqiq al-'Id on this hadith as an example of the Prophet's ﷺ jawami' al-kalim, his concise, comprehensive speech.

(1)

Leaving Excess in the Permissible

Leaving can apply to permissible things as well as sins. Leaving the haram is necessary, but leaving excess in permissible pursuits can be especially effective for spiritual elevation. Excess fills the soul, consumes time that cannot be recovered, and limits one's wayfaring. Eating too much is an ordinary good become excessive; preoccupation with celebrities is excessive in itself. The Shaykh also mentioned, from a spiritual rather than strictly fiqh angle, that some things certain jurists consider lawful may still affect a person's spirituality.

Not every permissible thing is to be left: quality time with family, beautiful clothing, and perfume are goods that need not displace obligations. The criticism is of excess that crowds out what deserves more attention.

The hadith ties to ihsan, awareness that Allah sees you, and to haya before Him, which produces restraint that feeds further restraint. Ask why you are spending the life and resources Allah gave you, your time, money, and mental energy, on a given matter, and whether He would love them directed elsewhere. None of this means ignoring social duties, refusing help, or avoiding genuine questions: a question needed for one's religion, responsibilities, study, or work is itself one's concern.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that not every halal activity is to be left. Excess is blamed when it displaces more important duties or a better use of limited resources.

(1)

Benefits

  • The measure of what concerns a Muslim is the Sharia, not desire or a modern slogan.
  • Leaving what is irrelevant must be paired with fulfilling duties and pursuing real benefit.
  • Restraint in excessive permissible things can raise a person spiritually when they crowd out greater priorities.
  • The hadith never excuses neglect of family, social, religious, or learning responsibilities.
  • Time and resources should be weighed against Allah's pleasure and the hereafter.

From Class: Firm Message, Gentle Delivery

The class discussed how to keep the message clear while treating people with wisdom. The Shaykh cited the command to call to the way of one's Lord with wisdom and good exhortation, and Allah's instruction to Musa to speak gently to Pharaoh. The message belongs to Allah and His Messenger ﷺ and cannot be altered; gentleness is required in how people are approached. A strong, direct response is sometimes appropriate, but it must stay within the Sharia and goodwill, without slander or divisiveness. He compared the way of many of his Azhari teachers, modeling what is right, with the more direct way associated with Hudhayfah, who asked the Prophet ﷺ about evil in order to avoid it. Both require wisdom and strength, and patience with those who slander can take more strength than answering in kind.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that the message itself is not to be altered, while wisdom and gentleness are required in how it is delivered.
