When the Outranked Outranks: Relative Strength and Hadith in Fiqh

When the Outranked Outranks

Right after the seven ranks of sahih (see Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim), Ibn Hajar adds that those ranks hold only from one angle. Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin explained this point in class: degrees of soundness matter both broadly and at a fine-grained level.

Text
أَمَّا لَوْ رَجَحَ قِسْمٌ عَلَى مَا هُوَ فَوْقَهُ بِأُمُورٍ أُخْرَى تَقْتَضِي التَّرْجِيحَ؛ فَإِنَّهُ يُقَدَّمُ عَلَى مَا فَوْقَهُ؛ إِذْ قَدْ يَعْرِضُ لِلْمَفُوقِ مَا يَجْعَلُهُ فَائِقاً.
كَمَا لَوْ كَانَ الحَدِيثُ عِنْدَ مُسْلِمٍ مَثَلاً، وَهُوَ مَشْهُورٌ قَاصِرٌ عَنْ دَرَجَةِ التَّوَاتُرِ، لَكِنْ حَفَّتْهُ قَرِينَةٌ صَارَ بِهَا يُفِيدُ العِلْمَ؛ فَإِنَّهُ يُقَدَّمُ عَلَى الحَدِيثِ الَّذِي يُخَرِّجُهُ البُخَارِيُّ إِذَا كَانَ فَرْداً مُطْلَقاً.
وَكَمَا لَوْ كَانَ الحَدِيثُ الَّذِي لَمْ يُخَرِّجَاهُ مِنْ تَرْجَمَةٍ وُصِفَتْ بِكَوْنِهَا أَصَحَّ الأَسَانِيدِ - كَمَالِكٍ، عَنْ نَافِعٍ، عَنِ ابْنِ عُمَرَ - فَإِنَّهُ يُقَدَّمُ عَلَى مَا انْفَرَدَ بِهِ أَحَدُهُمَا مَثَلاً، لَا سِيَّمَا إِذَا كَانَ فِي إِسْنَادِهِ مَنْ فِيهِ مَقَالٌ.

Translation
As for when a category outweighs what is above it through other matters that call for preference, it is given precedence over what is above it, since what is outranked may meet something that makes it outranking. For example, a hadith in Muslim that is mashhur, short of tawatur, but surrounded by an indicator through which it yields knowledge: it is given precedence over a hadith al-Bukhari includes if that one is an absolute fard. Or a hadith the two did not include, coming from a chain described as the soundest of chains, such as Malik from Nafi' from Ibn 'Umar: it is given precedence over what one of the two narrated alone, especially if that one's chain includes someone who has been criticized.

Ibn Hajar, Nuzhat al-Nazar, on when a lower rank is preferred over a higher one.

Commentary

The seven ranks compare reports only by whose conditions they meet. At the finer level, a report from the sound collections outside the two Sahihs may be preferred over a solitary report in one of them, depending on the relative degrees of soundness. Appearing in one of the two books does not end every comparison. Ibn Hajar's two examples draw on earlier lessons: a mashhur report surrounded by indicators that yield knowledge (see Ahad and Certainty), and a golden chain (see Asahh al-Asanid).

In the next lesson a student read this passage aloud and the Shaykh translated it. He first restated the seven ranks, describing the seventh as reports judged sound by another qualified imam, such as al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i, or Abu Dawud, without meeting the conditions of either Shaykh. Soundness is not confined to the two Sahihs or to any fixed list of collections, and qualified ijtihad in judging reports remains open.

Not a blanket claim. The first example does not mean that every report in Muslim outranks every report in al-Bukhari. It shows that breadth of routes and supporting indicators can outweigh the usual order in a particular comparison.

Knowledge through support. A report below tawatur can yield knowledge when indicators support it, but that knowledge comes through the support, not from the report alone. At more advanced levels, it is not treated identically to a report established in itself.

(1)

Mashhur. The Shaykh preferred "well-known" to "widespread," while noting that keeping the Arabic term may be more precise. Here it carries its technical meaning: three or more narrators, below tawatur.

Tarjama. Not "translation" here, but a known route: a particular isnad or configuration of narrators, such as Malik from Nafi' from Ibn 'Umar.

(2)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin described two axes for evaluating a report. The source axis asks where it was recorded and how it relates to the compilers' conditions. The reception axis asks how scholars received, assessed, and criticized that specific report. The source ranking never removes the need to examine the chain, the breadth of routes, corroborating indicators, and criticism specific to the report. Ibn Hajar gives two explicit examples precisely so the hierarchy is not taught as a rigid table. The Shaykh noted that al-Manawi's commentary warns jurists to examine further, especially corroborating factors, and recalled Ibn Hajar's warning not to dismiss reports merely because they are absent from al-Bukhari or Muslim.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that a faqih or hadith scholar benefits from the work of earlier imams but must still assess the individual report. Never dismiss a hadith simply because it is not in al-Bukhari or Muslim.

(3)

Accepted, But Not Acted Upon

The Shaykh said the value of this fine grading shows most clearly when two accepted reports appear to conflict. Fiqh distinguishes the accepted report that is acted upon from the accepted report that is not acted upon. Not acting on a report does not mean the jurist disputes its soundness. Put another way, thubut, a report being established, is necessary for a ruling but not sufficient.

He gave two examples. Abu Hanifa may differ from the apparent implication of a hadith without disputing that it is sound. Imam Malik may narrate a hadith in the Muwatta while his fiqh seems to go against what one might infer from it. These call for a deeper account of how accepted reports are weighed and applied, not a conclusion that the imams rejected them. The faqih works within a method received through his madhhab and teachers, reaching back to the Prophet ﷺ.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that judging the imams' legal reasoning without understanding the levels of soundness, and the other factors bearing on how a report is applied, leads people to misunderstand or react strongly to what the imams did.

Why Seek Hundreds of Thousands of Hadith?

A student asked why scholars memorized hundreds of thousands of hadith, when more reports would seem to bring more scrutiny, more confusion between names, and more chances to err.

The Shaykh answered that they were not accumulating reports for their own sake. The first motive was piety and reverence for the Prophet ﷺ: seeking what is reliably attributed to him, his statements, actions, and tacit approvals, in order to preserve, learn, apply, and transmit them, and to receive blessing through them. This held in every generation, and above all in the earliest.

Further scholarly aims followed. A scholar might gather more routes to reach the soundest transmission. A student might travel to a teacher of a higher generation, or to one with a better balance of riwaya and diraya, even if most of that teacher's hadith were nearly identical to what the student already had. The journey was still worthwhile for the teacher's transmission, understanding, authorization, or wisdom. Imam Ahmad, for example, spent more time with Imam al-Shafi'i, though he could have obtained some narrations through a source closer to the Prophet ﷺ by generation, because al-Shafi'i combined riwaya and diraya so strongly.

Riwaya. Transmission or narration of reports.

Diraya. Understanding and scholarly comprehension of reports.

A higher isnad. A chain with fewer generations between the transmitter and the source. It is often an advantage, but not automatically more authoritative in every case.

(1)

Scholars also grouped and categorized reports to assess and consolidate them, not merely to weaken them. Someone whose overriding concern was fear of erring by learning more would not have been occupied with hadith scholarship at all.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that the student must not pursue transmission while neglecting understanding.

(2)

Weak Hadith Across the Four Madhhabs

A student asked whether these four grades of authoritative hadith hold across the usul of all four madhhabs, or only in the presentation of the mutakallimun. Do the Hanafis, for example, use a weak hadith for a practice that is recommended but not binding, such as wiping the face after du'a? The Shaykh called this an important and central question.

His answer: the classification applies across the four madhhabs. Using a weak hadith to support a nonbinding sunnah does not contradict it, because a weak hadith cannot establish a wajib or a haram, while it may be used in fada'il al-a'mal.

RealmExamplesCan a weak hadith establish it?
Binding rulingsWajib, haram: a person can be sinful for violating them.No.
Fada'il al-a'malRecommended practices, where neither doing nor leaving them is sinful.It may be used.

Wiping the face after du'a. Not a practice for which someone can be called sinful for doing it or for leaving it. Doing it within salah is a separate question.

Saying as-salamu 'alaykum after salah. A beautiful sunnah supported by several pieces of evidence, including weak hadith. Neither the one who greets nor the one who does not is sinful.

(1)

Fada'il al-a'mal are the nonbinding parts of the Sharia, done in the hope of drawing nearer to the sunnah of the Prophet ﷺ and becoming better in character. When scholars say a weak hadith is not authoritative in fiqh, they mean it cannot establish what is binding. The subject is nuanced and governed by several principles.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that people may follow different understandings in nonbinding matters, and should not make these differences a cause of dispute.

(2)

Imam Ahmad and the Term "Hasan"

The Hanbali madhhab may look like an exception, since it is well known there that a weak hadith may be preferred when no stronger evidence exists. The Shaykh said this must be read with the Hanbali ranking of evidence in view: in the account he gave of Imam Ahmad's legal theory, a statement of a Companion ranks above a weak hadith.

There is also a question of terms. In Imam Ahmad's time, the two broad labels in common use were sahih and da'if. Hasan was not yet in wide circulation as a technical term; it was Imam al-Tirmidhi who later helped establish and popularize it. The idea that sound and weak reports have different ranks was understood before the term spread. So some scholars read Imam Ahmad's "da'if" in this context as referring to what would later be called hasan.

Weak Reports and the Essentials of Belief

The Shaykh distinguished binding authority in the essentials of belief (usul al-'aqa'id) from nonessential matters. A weak report containing a metaphorical or anthropomorphic-sounding image does not make a literal reading an essential article of belief, and does not justify declaring the wider Muslim community unbelievers. He described hasan li-ghayrihi through sahih li-dhatihi as authoritative evidence in the essentials of belief and in legal matters, with further detailed considerations in 'aqidah.

Coming Up: Applying the Terms

A student asked when the class would work through case studies, taking a hadith known to be sahih or da'if and analyzing it with the terms being learned. The Shaykh said application develops in levels. He would send a homework assignment suited to the class's current level, and it would not yet ask students to judge an isnad, which would be too early.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that practical training matters alongside theory, even early in the study of hadith sciences.
