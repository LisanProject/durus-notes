Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim: Comparison and the Ranks of Sahih

Three Levels of Agreement Between the Two Shaykhs

Before ranking the two books against each other, Ibn Hajar first ranks hadith by how much the two Shaykhs (Bukhari and Muslim) actually agree on a given report.

وَيَلْتَحِقُ بِهَذَا التَّفَاضُلِ: مَا اتَّفَقَ الشَّيْخَانِ عَلَى تَخْرِيجِهِ بِالنِّسْبَةِ إِلَى مَا انْفَرَدَ بِهِ أَحَدُهُمَا، وَمَا انْفَرَدَ بِهِ البُخَارِيُّ بِالنِّسْبَةِ إِلَى مَا انْفَرَدَ بِهِ مُسْلِمٌ؛ لِاتِّفَاقِ العُلَمَاءِ بَعْدَهُمَا عَلَى تَلَقِّي كِتَابَيْهِمَا بِالقَبُولِ وَاخْتِلَافِهِمْ فِي أَيِّهِمَا أَرْجَحُ، فَمَا اتَّفَقَا عَلَيْهِ أَرْجَحُ مِنْ هَذِهِ الحَيْثِيَّةِ مِمَّا لَمْ يَتَّفِقَا عَلَيْهِ.
Joined to this ranking is: what the two Shaykhs agreed upon narrating, relative to what either one narrated alone, and what al-Bukhari narrated alone, relative to what Muslim narrated alone, since the scholars after them agreed on receiving both their books with acceptance, while disagreeing over which of the two is preferred. So what they agreed upon is preferred, from this angle, over what they did not agree upon.

Ibn Hajar, Nuzhat al-Nazar, on the levels of agreement between the two Sahihs.

وَقَدْ صَرَّحَ الجُمْهُورُ بِتَقْدِيمِ صَحِيحِ البُخَارِيِّ فِي الصِّحَّةِ، وَلَمْ يُوجَدْ عَنْ أَحَدٍ التَّصْرِيحُ بِنَقِيضِهِ.
The majority have explicitly stated that Sahih al-Bukhari is given precedence in soundness, and no one has been found explicitly stating the opposite of that.

Ibn Hajar, on the scholarly consensus preferring al-Bukhari.

Al-Nisaburi's Saying, and What It Does Not Actually Mean

A well-known saying is sometimes read as though it puts Muslim's book ahead of Bukhari's. Ibn Hajar shows this is a misreading.

وَأَمَّا مَا نُقِلَ بِهِ عَنْ أَبِي عَلِيٍّ النَّيْسَابُورِيِّ أَنَّهُ قَالَ: "مَا تَحْتَ أَدِيمِ السَّمَاءِ أَصَحُّ مِنْ كِتَابِ مُسْلِمٍ"؛ فَلَمْ يُصَرِّحْ بِكَوْنِهِ أَصَحَّ مِنْ صَحِيحِ البُخَارِيِّ؛ لِأَنَّهُ إِنَّمَا نَفَى وُجُودَ أَصَحَّ مِنْ كِتَابِ مُسْلِمٍ، إِذِ المَنْفِيُّ إِنَّمَا هُوَ مَا تَقْتَضِيهِ صِيغَةُ "أَفْعَلَ" مِنْ زِيَادَةِ صِحَّةٍ شَارَكَ فِيهِ صَحِيحَ مُسْلِمٍ فِي الصِّحَّةِ فَيَمْتَازُ بِتِلْكَ الزِّيَادَةِ عَلَيْهِ، وَلَمْ يَنْفِ المُسَاوَاةَ.
As for what has been related from Abu 'Ali al-Naysaburi, that he said, "There is nothing under the canopy of heaven more sound than the book of Muslim," he did not thereby state that it is more sound than Sahih al-Bukhari. He only denied the existence of something more sound than Muslim's book, since what is negated is only whatever the comparative form ("more sound") would require, namely something that shares in soundness with Sahih Muslim while surpassing it by an added degree of soundness. He did not negate equality with it.

Ibn Hajar, on al-Naysaburi's statement and its logical scope.

In other words, "nothing is more sound than Muslim's book" is fully compatible with "Bukhari's book is equally sound, or even more sound." Al-Naysaburi's statement only rules out something surpassing Muslim; it says nothing at all about whether Bukhari's book equals or exceeds it.

The Maghribi View: An Argument About Style, Not Soundness

وَكَذَلِكَ مَا نُقِلَ عَنْ بَعْضِ المَغَارِبَةِ أَنَّهُ فَضَّلَ صَحِيحَ مُسْلِمٍ عَلَى صَحِيحِ البُخَارِيِّ؛ فَذَلِكَ فِيمَا يَرْجِعُ إِلَى حُسْنِ السِّيَاقِ، وَجَوْدَةِ الوَضْعِ وَالتَّرْتِيبِ. وَلَمْ يُفْصِحْ أَحَدٌ مِنْهُمْ بِأَنَّ ذَلِكَ رَاجِعٌ إِلَى الأَصَحِّيَّةِ، وَلَوْ أَفْصَحُوا بِذَلِكَ لَرُدَّ عَلَيْهِمْ.
Likewise what has been related from some of the Maghribi scholars, that they preferred Sahih Muslim over Sahih al-Bukhari, that preference goes back to the quality of its narrative flow and the excellence of its arrangement and ordering. None of them made it explicit that this preference goes back to greater soundness, and had they made that explicit, it would have been refuted.

Ibn Hajar, on the Maghribi preference for Muslim's arrangement.

This preference, in other words, was always about readability and structure, Muslim's book is famous for grouping all the routes of a single hadith together in one place rather than scattering them across chapters the way Bukhari's is. It was never a claim that Muslim's book is more authentic.

Three Grounds for Preferring Bukhari

Beyond general reception, Ibn Hajar gives three specific technical grounds on which al-Bukhari's book is preferred.

فَأَمَّا رُجْحَانُهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ الِاتِّصَالِ: فَلِاشْتِرَاطِهِ أَنْ يَكُونَ الرَّاوِي قَدْ ثَبَتَ لَهُ لِقَاءُ مَنْ رَوَى عَنْهُ وَلَوْ مَرَّةً، وَاكْتَفَى مُسْلِمٌ بِمُطْلَقِ المُعَاصَرَةِ.
As for its preference in terms of ittisal (connection): this is because he requires that the narrator be established to have actually met the one he narrates from, even if only once, while Muslim was satisfied with mere contemporaneity.

Ibn Hajar, the first ground: ittisal.

وَأُلْزِمَ البُخَارِيُّ بِأَنَّهُ يَحْتَاجُ إِلَى أَنْ لَا يَقْبَلَ العَنْعَنَةَ أَصْلاً! وَمَا أُلْزِمَ بِهِ لَيْسَ بِلَازِمٍ؛ لِأَنَّ الرَّاوِيَ إِذَا ثَبَتَ لَهُ اللِّقَاءُ مَرَّةً، لَا يَجْرِي فِي رِوَايَاتِهِ احْتِمَالُ أَنْ لَا يَكُونَ سَمِعَ؛ لِأَنَّهُ يَلْزَمُ مِنْ جَرَيَانِهِ أَنْ يَكُونَ مُدَلِّساً، وَالمَسْأَلَةُ مَفْرُوضَةٌ فِي غَيْرِ المُدَلِّسِ.
Bukhari has been challenged with the claim that his own condition would require him to reject the "'an" formula entirely, but this objection does not actually follow: once a narrator is established to have met his source even once, the possibility that he did not hear a given report from him no longer applies to his narrations, since for that possibility to apply he would have to be a mudallis (one who conceals a break in the chain), and the case under discussion presumes a narrator who is not a mudallis.

Ibn Hajar, rebutting the objection that Bukhari's own standard would reject an'ana.

وَأَمَّا رُجْحَانُهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ العَدَالَةِ وَالضَّبْطِ: فَلِأَنَّ الرِّجَالَ الَّذِينَ تُكُلِّمَ فِيهِمْ مِنْ رِجَالِ مُسْلِمٍ أَكْثَرُ عَدَداً مِنَ الرِّجَالِ الَّذِينَ تُكُلِّمَ فِيهِمْ مِنْ رِجَالِ البُخَارِيِّ، مَعَ أَنَّ البُخَارِيَّ لَمْ يُكْثِرْ مِنْ إِخْرَاجِ حَدِيثِهِمْ، بَلْ غَالِبُهُمْ مِنْ شُيُوخِهِ الَّذِينَ أَخَذَ عَنْهُمْ وَمَارَسَ حَدِيثَهُمْ، بِخِلَافِ مُسْلِمٍ فِي الأَمْرَيْنِ.
As for its preference in terms of 'adalah and dabt: the narrators criticized among Muslim's men are greater in number than the narrators criticized among Bukhari's men, despite the fact that Bukhari did not narrate extensively from them, most of them being his own shaykhs, from whom he took directly and whose narrations he had thoroughly practiced and examined, unlike Muslim in both of these respects.

Ibn Hajar, the second ground: 'adalah and dabt.

وَأَمَّا رُجْحَانُهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ عَدَمِ الشُّذُوذِ وَالإِعْلَالِ: فَلِأَنَّ مَا انْتُقِدَ عَلَى البُخَارِيِّ مِنَ الأَحَادِيثِ أَقَلُّ عَدَداً مِمَّا انْتُقِدَ عَلَى مُسْلِمٍ، هَذَا مَعَ اتِّفَاقِ العُلَمَاءِ عَلَى أَنَّ البُخَارِيَّ كَانَ أَجَلَّ مِنْ مُسْلِمٍ فِي العُلُومِ، وَأَعْرَفَ بِصِنَاعَةِ الحَدِيثِ مِنْهُ، وَأَنَّ مُسْلِماً تِلْمِيذُهُ وَخِرِّيجُهُ، وَلَمْ يَزَلْ يَسْتَفِيدُ مِنْهُ وَيَتَّبِعُ أَثَارَهُ، حَتَّى قَالَ الدَّارَقُطْنِيُّ: "لَوْلَا البُخَارِيُّ لَمَا رَاحَ مُسْلِمٌ وَلَا جَاءَ!"
As for its preference in terms of freedom from shudhudh and i'lal: the hadiths criticized against Bukhari are fewer in number than those criticized against Muslim, and this is alongside the scholars' agreement that Bukhari surpassed Muslim in knowledge and was more versed than him in the craft of hadith, and that Muslim was his student and product, continually benefiting from him and following in his footsteps, so much so that al-Daraqutni said: "Were it not for al-Bukhari, Muslim would not have gone nor come!"

Ibn Hajar, the third ground: freedom from shudhudh and i'lal, closing with al-Daraqutni's statement.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin illustrated the second and third grounds with real figures: Bukhari's Sahih contains roughly 480 narrators, of whom about 80 have been criticized, while Muslim's Sahih contains roughly 620 narrators, of whom about 160 have been criticized. He also walked through examples of hadith found in both collections and hadith unique to each, and gave historical context on the movement from oral to written transmission across the early generations, framing these technical details as carrying real spiritual weight rather than only academic interest.

An'ana and Tadlis

An'ana: use of the word "'an" ("from") in a chain instead of an explicit active form like "sami'tu" ("I heard"), which on its own leaves some ambiguity about whether the narration was actually heard directly.

(1)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin explained tadlis as the specific danger an'ana raises: a narrator concealing an actual break in his chain by wording his narration ambiguously rather than admitting he did not hear the report directly from the person he narrates it from. This is exactly the concern Bukhari's stricter liqa' condition is built to guard against more tightly than Muslim's mu'asarah condition does.

Does This Ranking Extend Beyond the Two Sahihs?

A student asked whether this entire ranking system applies only when comparing Bukhari and Muslim to each other, or whether it extends to judging other canonical collections such as al-Tirmidhi's.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin confirmed that it extends further: later scholars judged narrations in other collections by whether they met the criteria Bukhari and Muslim had established, using those two conditions as a benchmark even outside the two Sahihs themselves. He referenced al-Hakim's al-Mustadrak specifically, a work built entirely around collecting hadith that meet the Sahihayn's conditions without having been included in either book.

(1)

A second question followed: can chains relying on an'ana still reach the level of mutawatir, or does the ambiguity of "'an" cap them below that threshold permanently?

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin's answer was no, not necessarily: when narrators known to be trustworthy and known not to practice tadlis use "'an," it functions in practice the same as if they had specified the mode of narration explicitly. Mutawatir status can still be reached through such chains if the isnad's integrity and the narrators' reliability are independently established; the ambiguity of the wording is not an automatic ceiling.

(2)

The Six Ranks of Sahih, and a Seventh Outside Them

With the two books ranked against each other, Ibn Hajar extends the same logic to hadith found in neither book, ranking them by whether and how they meet the two Shaykhs' conditions.

وَمِنْ ثَمَّ: أَيْ وَمِنْ هَذِهِ الحَيْثِيَّةِ - وَهِيَ أَرْجَحِيَّةُ - مَا قُدِّمَ (صَحِيحُ البُخَارِيِّ) عَلَى غَيْرِهِ مِنَ الكُتُبِ المُصَنَّفَةِ فِي الحَدِيثِ. ثُمَّ صَحِيحُ (مُسْلِمٍ)؛ لِمُشَارَكَتِهِ لِلْبُخَارِيِّ فِي اتِّفَاقِ العُلَمَاءِ عَلَى تَلَقِّي كِتَابِهِ بِالقَبُولِ أَيْضاً، سِوَى مَا عُلِّلَ.
And from this, that is, from this consideration of preference, Sahih al-Bukhari is given precedence over every other book compiled in hadith. Then Sahih Muslim, for sharing with al-Bukhari in the scholars' agreement to receive his book with acceptance as well, aside from what has been shown to be defective.

Ibn Hajar, ranking the two books themselves as the first two tiers.

ثُمَّ يُقَدَّمُ فِي الأَرْجَحِيَّةِ مِنْ حَيْثُ الأَصَحِّيَّةِ مَا وَافَقَهُ شَرْطُهُمَا؛ لِأَنَّ المُرَادَ بِهِ رُوَاتُهُمَا مَعَ بَاقِي شُرُوطِ الصَّحِيحِ. فَإِنْ كَانَ الخَبَرُ عَلَى شَرْطِهِمَا مَعاً؛ كَانَ دُونَ مَا أَخْرَجَهُ مُسْلِمٌ أَوْ مِثْلَهُ. وَإِنْ كَانَ عَلَى شَرْطِ أَحَدِهِمَا؛ فَيُقَدَّمُ شَرْطُ البُخَارِيِّ وَحْدَهُ عَلَى شَرْطِ مُسْلِمٍ وَحْدَهُ تَبَعاً لِأَصْلِ كُلٍّ مِنْهُمَا.
Then, in terms of preponderance regarding soundness, what agrees with both of their conditions is given precedence, because what is meant by it is their narrators together with the remaining conditions of sahih. If a report matches both of their conditions together, it ranks below what Muslim included, or its equivalent. And if it matches only one of their conditions, then Bukhari's condition alone is given precedence over Muslim's condition alone, following from the underlying standing of each of the two.

Ibn Hajar, ranking hadith outside the two books by which conditions they meet.

فَخَرَجَ لَنَا مِنْ هَذَا سِتَّةُ أَقْسَامٍ تَتَفَاوَتُ دَرَجَاتُهَا فِي الصِّحَّةِ، وَثَمَّ قِسْمٌ سَابِعٌ؛ وَهُوَ مَا لَيْسَ عَلَى شَرْطِهِمَا اجْتِمَاعاً وَانْفِرَاداً، وَهَذَا التَّفَاوُتُ إِنَّمَا هُوَ بِالنَّظَرِ إِلَى الحَيْثِيَّةِ المَذْكُورَةِ.
From this we arrive at six categories, whose degrees vary in soundness, and then a seventh category: what is on neither of their conditions, jointly or individually. This variance is only with respect to the particular consideration mentioned.

Ibn Hajar, summarizing the six-plus-one classification.

1. What both Bukhari and Muslim agreed upon narrating.

2. What al-Bukhari narrated alone.

3. What Muslim narrated alone.

4. What meets both their conditions together but was not included in either book.

5. What meets only al-Bukhari's condition alone.

6. What meets only Muslim's condition alone.

(1)

7. What meets neither of their specific conditions, whether taken jointly or individually, but may still be judged sahih on other grounds entirely. This is a difference of consideration, not a claim that this seventh category is unsound.

(2)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin clarified that works like al-Hakim's al-Mustadrak were compiled specifically to gather hadith falling into category four above, material meeting both Shaykhs' conditions without having been included in either of their actual books. He also walked through how to test whether a hadith found in a collection like al-Tirmidhi's or al-Nasa'i's genuinely matches Bukhari's or Muslim's criteria, rather than assuming it does simply because it appears in a well-known collection.

A Note on Scholarly Humility

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin closed with an aside on how Bukhari and Muslim related to each other as scholars: although Bukhari is preferred on the technical grounds above, benefit and knowledge flowed both ways between them in life, and no single scholar stands above learning from another. He stressed that these technical rankings should never overshadow humility and gratitude toward all the hadith scholars who preserved this material, al- Daraqutni's own statement above being itself a model of that humility, crediting Bukhari's influence even while praising Muslim.
