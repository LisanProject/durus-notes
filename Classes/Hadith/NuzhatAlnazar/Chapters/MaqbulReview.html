Review of Chapter 3: The Accepted Hadith

How to use this page The class spent a lesson reviewing this chapter, and this page follows that review. Recall each list before reading it, then work through "Asking Why," the mustalah counterpart of ta'lil: what each clause of a definition excludes, and what each judgment rests on. The cases at the bottom are the real test.

The Shaykh's Five Questions

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin gave these as the order of thinking whenever a hadith is judged:

  1. What exactly is being judged: the hadith, one route, or one segment?
  2. What is the initial status of the route?
  3. Are there conflicting or additional versions?
  4. What do those variants reveal?
  5. What conclusion follows from comparing them?

He also reviewed the order of study: reports were first divided by transmission (Chapter 2), accepted reports were then divided by grade, then examined for variants, and finally searched for support through mutaba'at and shawahid. Transmission and soundness can affect each other, but they are separate judgments: mashhur describes spread, not authenticity.

Five conditions of sahih li-dhatihi
  • 'Adalah: a disposition to maintain taqwa and muru'ah.
  • Tamm al-dabt: of memory (sadr) or of the written record (kitab).
  • Ittisal: no drop anywhere in the chain.
  • No 'illah: no hidden, impairing defect.
  • No shudhudh: no contradiction of someone more reliable.

Ranks and Comparisons

The golden chains, in three tiers
  • First: al-Zuhri, from Salim, from Ibn 'Umar; Ibn Sirin, from 'Ubaydah, from 'Ali; al-Nakha'i, from 'Alqamah, from Ibn Mas'ud.
  • Second: Buraid, from Abu Burdah, from Abu Musa; Hammad ibn Salamah, from Thabit, from Anas.
  • Third: Suhayl, from Abu Salih, from Abu Hurayrah; al-'Ala', from his father, from Abu Hurayrah.
  • Below them, hasan: Ibn Ishaq, from 'Asim, from Jabir; 'Amr ibn Shu'ayb, from his father, from his grandfather.
The seven ranks relative to the two Sahihs

I'tibar, Mutaba'ah, and Shahid

I'tibar is the whole process of tracing a report through the collections, the jawami', masanid, and ajza', to see whether a report thought to be solitary has support. Mutaba'ah and shahid are what that process may find; they are not alternatives to it.

TermMeaning
Mutaba'ahAnother route for the report through the same Companion.
Mutaba'ah tammahComplete: the support reaches the narrator himself.
Mutaba'ah qasirahPartial: the routes meet higher up in the isnad.
ShahidA supporting report through a different Companion.
What i'tibar can produce
  • Strengthening, when the conditions for it are met.
  • Clarified meaning, as one text explains another: Quran by Quran, Quran by hadith, hadith by hadith.
  • A changed description without a changed grade: hasan gharib becoming hasan mashhur.
  • A way to decide between variant versions.
  • An exposed defect: the "support" can reveal a problem in the original instead of repairing it.

Asking Why: What Each Judgment Rests On

Why does sahih have ranks at all? Because the qualities it rests on, 'adalah and dabt above all, come in degrees, and soundness is a judgment of preponderant probability, so stronger qualities give a stronger preponderance.

(1)

Why is al-Bukhari preferred over Muslim? On three technical grounds: ittisal (he required that meeting be established at least once, while Muslim accepted contemporaneity), 'adalah and dabt (fewer criticized narrators, mostly his own shaykhs whom he knew closely), and fewer reports criticized for shudhudh and i'lal.

(2)

Why can a report in Muslim outrank one in al-Bukhari? Because the seven ranks compare only by whose conditions are met. If a mashhur report in Muslim is surrounded by qara'in that yield knowledge and the one in al-Bukhari is fard mutlaq, the other factors decide.

(3)

Why does corroboration lift a light weakness but not a heavy one? Because support can make up a shortfall that leaves the narrator's truthfulness intact, but it cannot cure a defect such as lying or severe error. Many routes sharing the same severe defect are still defective.

(4)

Why doesn't a supporting route change the first route's own grade? Because the support joins the evidence; it does not rewrite the route. What rises is the judgment drawn from the whole, and a single route rises only after its own examination.

(5)

Why is "hasan sahih" from a single route below plain sahih? Because it records hesitation between two judgments of one narrator, and a categorical judgment is stronger than a hesitant one. From two routes it means both, and the extra route strengthens it.

(6)

Why can't every addition by a reliable narrator be accepted? Because sahih requires the absence of shudhudh, which is precisely a reliable narrator contradicting someone more reliable. Accepting every addition would empty that condition of meaning.

(7)

Why doesn't one rejected report make a narrator unreliable? Because thiqah means generally reliable, not infallible. A narrator may transmit by meaning, add, or omit; each report is checked on its own, and a single error leaves his standing intact.

(8)

Why doesn't a connected version always beat a disconnected one? Because connecting or dropping a link is a technical claim like any other. Abu Hatim preferred Ibn 'Uyaynah's connected version on the evidence of that case, not on a rule.

(9)

Why is a marfu' version not automatically stronger than a mawquf one? Because raising a report is also a claim to be tested. A weak narrator can raise what reliable narrators preserve as a Companion's words, which makes the raised version munkar.

Case 1A reliable narrator alone narrates a hadith from his teacher, and no other version exists. Is it shadh?

No. There is nothing to contradict, so it is gharib. Shadh needs a conflicting version from someone more reliable.

Case 2Two reliable versions: one lists two instructions, the other three. The third doesn't negate the first two. What happens?

The addition is accepted like an independent report. There is no conflict, so there is nothing to weigh.

Case 3A student finds a second route for a hadith and declares the first route now sahih li-dhatihi. Correct him.

The first route keeps its own grade. At most the hadith as a whole may become sahih li-ghayrihi, and only if the support meets its conditions. The second route might even expose a defect instead.

Case 4A weak narrator attributes a statement to the Prophet ﷺ; several reliable narrators give the same words as Ibn 'Abbas's. Name both versions.

The raised version is munkar; the version stopping at Ibn 'Abbas is ma'ruf, as in Abu Hatim's example.

Case 5Al-Tirmidhi calls a hadith "hasan gharib, we know it only from this route." Does this contradict his rule that hasan comes through more than one route?

No. His definition covers only what he labels "hasan" with nothing added. He did not define his combined labels.

Case 6A hadith is sahih, yet Imam Malik did not rule by its apparent meaning. Did he reject it?

Not necessarily. A report can be accepted but not acted upon in a ruling, through reconciliation, abrogation, specification, qualification, or preference within his method.

Case 7I'tibar on a hasan gharib hadith finds a second route of similar strength. Has its grade changed?

Not necessarily. It may simply stop being gharib, becoming hasan mashhur in description while staying hasan in grade.

Questions to Bring to the Shaykh

  • When the routes are judged sahih li-ghayrihi together, what exactly does a scholar cite when he quotes just one of them?
  • How did the early critics' broader use of munkar differ in practice from Ibn Hajar's definition?
  • In al-Shafi'i's criterion, does a shorter version from a non-hafiz always count in his favor, or only when it agrees with the hafiz in what it does say?
  • Which grounds of preference (precision, number, specialization in a teacher, a written book) usually carry the most weight when they point in different directions?
