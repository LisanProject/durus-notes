The Four Grades Revisited: Intrinsic and External Strength

Ibn Hajar's Four-Way Division, in His Words

The class returned to the definition of sahih li-dhatihi (see Sahih li-Dhatihi and the Divisions of Accepted Hadith) and read Ibn Hajar's explanation of how it opens the division of the accepted report into four types.

Text
وَهَذَا أَوَّلُ تَقْسِيمِ المَقْبُولِ إِلَى أَرْبَعَةِ أَنْوَاعٍ؛ لِأَنَّهُ إِمَّا أَنْ يَشْتَمِلَ مِنْ صِفَاتِ القَبُولِ عَلَى أَعْلَاهَا أَوْ لَا:
الأَوَّلُ: الصَّحِيحُ لِذَاتِهِ.
وَالثَّانِي: إِنْ وُجِدَ مَا يَجْبُرُ ذَلِكَ القُصُورَ كَكَثْرَةِ الطُّرُقِ؛ فَهُوَ الصَّحِيحُ أَيْضاً؛ لَكِنْ لَا لِذَاتِهِ.

Translation
This is the first division of the accepted report into four types, because it either contains the qualities of acceptance at their highest or it does not. The first is sahih li-dhatihi. The second: if something is found that compensates for that shortfall, such as a multiplicity of routes, then it is also sahih, but not in itself. Where there is no compensation, it is hasan li-dhatihi. And if an indicator arises that makes acceptance preponderant in a report one would otherwise suspend judgment on, then it is also hasan, but not in itself. The discussion of sahih li-dhatihi was put first because of the height of its rank.

Ibn Hajar, Nuzhat al-Nazar, on the four types of the accepted report.

Commentary

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin stressed that hasan li-dhatihi meets the same structural conditions as sahih, at a slightly lower degree. The shortfall is usually most visible in precision, but in the next lesson he clarified that it can occur in any of the five conditions, sometimes very subtly. It is still accepted, authoritative, and usable as proof, even though it ranks below sahih. Hasan also has internal ranks of its own: not every hasan is equal in strength.

Ghalabat al-Zann (Preponderant Probability). Calling a report sahih is a judgment of graded preponderant probability, not an automatic claim of absolute certainty. Because the qualities that produce soundness vary in strength, accepted reports occupy different ranks. This ties back to the earlier discussion in Islamic Epistemology.

(1)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that hasan is not a kind of weak hadith. It is accepted and authoritative; it simply ranks below sahih.

(2)

Li-Dhatihi and Li-Ghayrihi: What Can Rise, and How Far

Li-dhatihi means the route, taken with its own evidence, reaches the grade without needing anything else to cover a shortfall. Li-ghayrihi means external corroborating evidence has joined the evidence of the route. The final grade then belongs to the combined configuration of evidence.

GradeWhat it isCan it rise?
Sahih li-dhatihiAll five conditions at the highest level of precision.Already the highest grade; needs no support.
Hasan li-dhatihiSame conditions, with the narrator's precision slightly lighter.Yes, to sahih li-ghayrihi, if adequate external support compensates.
Da'if, weakness not severeFalls short, but the deficiency is remediable.Yes, to hasan li-ghayrihi, through corroborating evidence.
Da'if, weakness severeFalls short in a way that cannot be mended.No. More equally weak routes do not remove the problem.

The Shaykh described two ways a hasan li-dhatihi report reaches sahih li-ghayrihi: a single hasan route strengthened by outside corroboration, or several routes, each hasan li-dhatihi on its own, that strengthen one another so that together they are judged sahih li-ghayrihi.

External evidence joins; it does not erase. When routes strengthen one another, what is elevated is the knowledge drawn from their totality. Sometimes a particular route is also elevated, but never automatically: each route needs its own examination.

The hadith, the route, and the segment. Distinguish the hadith as a whole from any one report or route carrying it. Different routes may include or omit phrases, and a hadith may be sound except for one segment, which may only be hasan.

(1)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that a report's status is not a mathematical calculation. The external factors scholars use are known and specific, not arbitrary, and counting routes is never enough by itself.

(2)

The Five Conditions as Five Lenses

The Shaykh called examining a hadith the laboratory work of hadith criticism, with each of the five conditions acting as a separate lens. (The conditions themselves are defined in The Conditions of Sahih li-Dhatihi Explained.) Each lens is independent and equally important: passing one never substitutes for another.

'Adalah is not dabt. A narrator can be upright and still lack complete precision. When scholars notice a shortfall in precision, they scrutinize that narrator's reports more closely. Self-criticism by narrators, and criticism by their peers and later generations, both feed into this.

'Adalah in hadith versus testimony. In hadith, 'adalah concerns the narrator's piety and propriety in relation to transmitting from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, not only the general legal trustworthiness asked of an eyewitness.

(1)

Dabt is not infallibility. Precision is the capacity to transmit as heard, through retained memory or a protected, accurate written record. It does not make error impossible, and the possibility of error is one reason hadith criticism exists at all.

(2)

Ittisal is judged on its own. A break in the chain does not by itself show that a narrator lacks uprightness. And an apparent omission is not always a true omission; it needs further examination. Later lessons cover the evidence of meeting and hearing in detail.

(3)

Shudhudh turns on contradiction: the report must not contradict a stronger transmission.

'Illah turns on two words: hidden and impairing. Not every defect is an 'illah, and not every unusual variation harms the report.

(4)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes caution for learners: a feature that looks to a student like an early hadith scholar's mistake may instead be something the student has not yet understood.

(5)

Class Exercise: Five Statements

The Shaykh gave the class five statements and asked how each relates to a hadith being sahih. A student asked whether "a highly regarded collection" meant only al-Bukhari and Muslim or also the Sunan; the Shaykh said he meant the sahih collections.

ExerciseHow does each of these relate to soundness? (1) No stronger transmitter contradicts the narration. (2) The report is in a highly regarded (sahih) collection. (3) There are three routes for the same hadith with a parallel meaning. (4) The chain is connected. (5) Every narrator is trustworthy.

The statements do not answer one question, so they should not be ranked against each other as rival tests of soundness. Each points to a different part of the examination:

(1) is about the absence of shudhudh, not precision, as the Shaykh clarified when a student asked. (2) suggests the report has met high standards across the conditions; the Shaykh especially tied inclusion in a sahih collection to confidence that hidden defects were examined. (3) concerns multiple routes and is closer to i'tibar: it gives the critic more material for finding corroboration, but the strengthening is not automatic. (4) states continuity directly. (5) concerns 'adalah and dabt, but cannot establish soundness alone.

Soundness is a matrix of conditions and evidence, not one impressive fact. "All the narrators are trustworthy, so the hadith is sahih" must be qualified: it can only be called sahih once continuity and freedom from shudhudh and 'illah are also confirmed.

Four Dimensions, and the Disciplines Built for Them

The five conditions examine four broad dimensions of a report, and each dimension grew its own field of study.

DimensionConditionsTools and disciplines
The narrators'Adalah, dabt'Ilm al-rijal, assessing uprightness and precision together and separately; musnad works, which group a narrator's reports so they can be compared.
Transmission eventsIttisalMeetings, hearing, the mode of transmission, formulas such as "I heard" and "from," and dates.
RoutesCorroborationTakhrij and the study of a hadith's routes.
The whole reportNo shudhudh, no 'illahComparing the wording and attribution against stronger wordings, attributions, routes, and evidence, then synthesizing before judging.

A reliable, pious narrator can still err in a particular report, which is why biographical criticism alone is never the end of the matter. If even one of the five conditions is not met at the required level, the report is not sahih li-dhatihi.

Sahih al-Isnad and Sahih al-Hadith

Discussion of the conditions of al-Bukhari and Muslim concerns sahih al-isnad, a sound chain, not every possible judgment of sahih al-hadith, a sound hadith. Everything in the two Sahihs is sound, but the two imams did not include every hadith that could be judged sound. A sound hadith may be absent because it does not meet their particular conditions for the isnad, or because they did not know that specific narration or variant.

Sahih al-Isnad. A technical judgment on the chain. Broadly, the first three conditions ('adalah, dabt, ittisal) bear most directly on it.

Sahih al-Hadith. A broader, more open judgment on the hadith as a whole, where the last two conditions (no shudhudh, no 'illah) compare the total report, its wording, and its routes.

(1)

The Shaykh added that hadith evaluation remains open to scholarly ijtihad. A later scholar may find additional routes or factors that establish a report as hasan or sound, even while one particular isnad for it stays hasan or da'if.

Da'if Is Not One Rank

In the broad sense, da'if covers whatever fails to meet the qualities of acceptance at the relevant level. But the causes, the severity, and whether the weakness can be remedied all differ, and they do not produce the same ruling.

Defects in 'adalah range from a limited unknown status, to significant unknownness, open sin, an accusation of lying, deliberate lying, and established fabrication.

Defects in dabt may be a mild shortfall, gross error, weakness specific to one teacher, or weakness specific to one period of the narrator's life, for instance strong retention early and weaker retention later, which scholars must identify.

Discontinuity may be visible or concealed, and must be checked to see whether it is truly an omission. Sometimes the dropped source is known and trustworthy, so the apparent gap does not act as a real defect.

(1)

A weakness that is not severe may be strengthened by adequate support, and even when it does not reach the full grade of hasan li-ghayrihi, the report may still be benefited from in a limited way depending on that support. Conversely, a severe defect is not removed by multiplying routes that share it.

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes that a purely mathematical approach to hadith is superficial and harmful. It leads people to dismiss weak reports without regard to their different causes and degrees, or to assume that many routes automatically mean acceptance.

(2)

A First Look at Mutaba'ah, Shahid, and I'tibar

These terms come later in the Nuzhat, but the Shaykh gave simplified definitions since they bear on how reports are strengthened.

Mutaba'ah. In the Shaykh's simplified explanation, an agreeing transmission through the same Companion. The two narrations may differ in strength, and the stronger may support the weaker, but further conditions apply and the strengthening is not automatic.

Shahid. A similar report, in wording and meaning or in meaning alone, transmitted from another Companion.

I'tibar. The systematic tracing of routes to see whether supporting mutaba'at or shawahid exist.

(1)

He closed by restating the general order: sahih is above hasan, intrinsic strength is distinct from external strengthening, and each grade has internal ranks, so a particular report may outrank another at its own level because of corroborating evidence. That point is taken up in When the Outranked Outranks, which also answers the class question of whether these four grades hold across all four madhhabs.

Homework Review: Refinements

The next lesson opened with the class answering review questions on this material, with the Shaykh adding to and correcting each answer. These points refine what is above.

Why Ibn Hajar starts with sahih li-dhatihi. It is the prototype: the model against which every other grade is understood by what it lacks or holds at a lower degree. That makes it the natural teaching starting point.

(1)

Hasan is accepted by ijma, not just by the jumhur. Accepting hasan li-dhatihi as independent evidence is unanimous, including the Hanafis, whom the Shaykh described as meticulous in accepting hadith.

Jumhur means the majority. Ijma means unanimous consensus. The two should not be confused.

(2)

Hasan yields probability, not 'ilm. Hasan li-dhatihi does not produce 'ilm, whether necessary or theoretical (nazari). It yields probabilistic knowledge, which can still be authoritative and come close to certainty.

Tawatur comes before grading. A mutawatir report sits beyond the ordinary criticism of a single chain. Only when a report is not mutawatir is its soundness examined, and sahih is the highest result of that examination. A sahih report is not necessarily mutawatir.

Qat'i is rare. A text that is decisive in both transmission and meaning is uncommon; scholars mostly work with probabilistic evidence of varying strength.

(3)

Sidi Shaykh Muhyiddin emphasizes translating sahih as "sound" rather than "authoritative" where "authoritative" might suggest the report is qat'i or mutawatir. Sahih and hasan are probabilistic, yet still respected and used in scholarship and law.

(4)

Gharib versus shadh. Gharib is about number: one narrator alone transmits the report at some point in the chain, at the root or later. Shadh is about contradiction: a narrator opposes someone weightier or more authoritative. A report can be gharib without being shadh.

(5)

Review 1A student says hasan li-dhatihi differs from sahih only in precision. Is that right?

Not quite. The difference is usually most noticeable in precision, but the slight shortfall can occur in any of the five conditions, and in the others it may be extremely subtle.

Review 2A hadith is graded hasan li-ghayrihi through several weak routes. Does that make each of those routes hasan?

No, not automatically. The knowledge drawn from the totality is elevated. A particular route may sometimes be elevated too, but only after its own examination.

Review 3If a report is established as sahih, does that settle the legal ruling?

No. Thubut (being established) is necessary but not sufficient. The full picture, including conflicting or stronger evidence, must be weighed. Abu Hanifa and Malik knew sound reports and still did not base particular opinions on them, for reasons they judged necessary. See Accepted, But Not Acted Upon.

Review 4Why does each grade have internal ranks?

Because the five conditions are themselves met in degrees, so two reports in the same broad grade can differ in strength. Weakness likewise differs by cause and severity: deliberate lying is not forgetfulness, and forgetfulness is not a single mistake.
