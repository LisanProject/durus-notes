Who Is Addressed by Command, and Al-Nahy

Text
اَلَّذِي يَدْخُلُ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَالنَّهْيِ وَمَا لَا يَدْخُلُ. يَدْخُلُ فِي خِطَابِ اللَّهِ تَعَالَى: ﴿الْمُؤْمِنُونَ﴾، وَالسَّاهِي وَالصَّبِيُّ وَالْمَجْنُونُ غَيْرُ دَاخِلِينَ.
Vocabulary
  • خِطَاب اللَّهkhitab Allah, the address of Allah, meaning the scope of who is actually being spoken to and held accountable by revealed command and prohibition
  • السَّاهِيal-sahi, one who is forgetful or unaware at the moment in question
  • الصَّبِيّal-sabi, a child who has not yet reached puberty
  • الْمَجْنُونal-majnun, a person who is insane
Translation
What falls under command and prohibition, and what does not. The believers fall under Allah's address. The forgetful, the child, and the insane do not fall under it.
Commentary

This section defines ahl al-taklif, who is actually addressed by a legal command or prohibition and therefore accountable to it, before the discussion of amr can move any further.

Who Is Addressed. Believers fall squarely under Allah's address, meaning they are the ones a command or prohibition is actually directed at and accountable to.

(1)

Who Is Excluded. Three categories are not addressed, and so are not held accountable in the moment: al-sahi (someone forgetful or unaware at that moment), al-sabi (a child before puberty), and al-majnun (someone insane). Exclusion here is not permanent for all three in the same way. Al-sahi is excluded only for the duration of the forgetfulness itself, and is required to make up an obligation, such as a missed prayer, once that awareness returns. This is a temporary lapse in address, not a standing exemption the way sabi and majnun are while the underlying condition lasts.

(2)

This distinction also shapes how a real fiqh dispute plays out. In the Shafi'i school, zakah is still owed on the wealth of a child or an insane person once it reaches nisab and a full year has passed, since the obligation is treated as attached to the wealth itself rather than to the taklif of its owner. The Hanafi school ties the obligation to bulugh (puberty) and 'aql (sanity) directly, and does not require zakah in that case. Both positions agree on who is and is not addressed by taklif in general; they differ on whether zakah specifically tracks the wealth or the person.

Text
وَالْكُفَّارُ مُخَاطَبُونَ بِفُرُوعِ الشَّرِيعَةِ، وَبِمَا لَا تَصِحُّ إِلَّا بِهِ، وَهُوَ الْإِسْلَامُ، لِقَوْلِهِ تَعَالَى: ﴿قَالُوا لَمْ نَكُ مِنَ الْمُصَلِّينَ﴾. وَالْأَمْرُ بِالشَّيْءِ نَهْيٌ عَنْ ضِدِّهِ، وَالنَّهْيُ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ أَمْرٌ بِضِدِّهِ.
Vocabulary
  • فُرُوع الشَّرِيعَةfuru' al-shari'ah, the branches of the law, meaning its specific practical rulings rather than its theological foundations
  • ضِدّdidd, the opposite of a given thing
Translation
Disbelievers are addressed by the branches of the law, and by that without which those branches cannot be valid, which is Islam, on account of Allah's statement: "They said, we were not among those who prayed." A command to a thing is a prohibition of its opposite, and a prohibition of a thing is a command to its opposite.
Commentary

Two more pieces close out this section: a clarification of how disbelievers relate to legal address despite falling outside the community of belief, and the reciprocal relationship between amr and nahy themselves.

Disbelievers and Furu' al-Shari'ah. Disbelievers are still addressed by the practical branches of the law, meaning they are genuinely blameworthy for not praying, not fasting, and so on, and not merely for disbelief in isolation. The verse cited has the people of the Fire naming their failure to pray specifically as one of the reasons for their punishment, alongside their disbelief, which only makes sense if they were genuinely addressed by that obligation in the first place. At the same time, none of those branches can actually be validly performed without the one precondition that makes any of them valid at all: Islam itself. A disbeliever is blameworthy for not praying, but cannot discharge that obligation by praying while still outside Islam.

(1)

Amr and Nahy Are Reciprocal. A command to do something functions, at the same time, as a prohibition of its opposite, and a prohibition of something functions as a command to its opposite. A command to speak the truth is, by the same act, a prohibition of lying; a prohibition of lying is, by the same act, a command to speak the truth. The two are not separate rulings that happen to align; they are two descriptions of a single ruling.

(2)

Why the Verse Counts as Real Evidence

An objection surfaces immediately. The verse only quotes what the people of the Fire say about themselves, so how can their own words serve as a dalil? The answer is that if their claim were false, Allah would have corrected it within the verse itself. Since no correction appears, the statement stands as accurate, and it genuinely establishes a ruling rather than merely reporting an opinion.

The fuller reasoning has a second layer. The verse shows the people of the Fire naming their failure to pray as one of the reasons for their punishment, on top of their kufr. That only makes sense if disbelievers had genuinely been addressed by the command to pray in the first place, since blame requires a prior address. From there the point generalizes. If they are shown to be addressed by one specific command, the same reasoning extends to every command and prohibition, since the underlying cause is the general scope of taklif itself, not something peculiar to salah.

The Point of the Address: Grounding Their Punishment

Being addressed by the furu' is not an empty technicality. Its entire benefit is that it makes punishment for neglecting them (iqab) sound in the first place. Punishment in the Hereafter for leaving obligations and committing what is forbidden is a direct consequence of taklif having actually been established in this world. Without a real address, there would be nothing to justly punish.

One View: Punished on Top of Kufr. One position, marked as rajih (preferred) in some commentary, holds that disbelievers are punished for the furu' specifically, in addition to and separately from their punishment for kufr itself.

(1)

A Refinement: Under the Divine Will. The more careful position holds this additional punishment is not automatic in every case, but falls under Allah's mashi'ah (will), on the strength of the general Qur'anic principle that Allah does not forgive shirk, but forgives what is less than that for whomever He wills.

(2)

Why the Furu' Aren't Valid From Them: Niyyah and Islam

Disbelievers are genuinely addressed by the furu', yet none of those acts can actually be performed validly by them while they remain in kufr. The reason is specific. Validity for most acts of worship depends on niyyah, a valid intention, and a sound niyyah in turn depends on Islam. A person outside Islam cannot form the kind of intention that makes an act of worship valid, since that is exactly what the underlying state of disbelief rules out, not the outward action itself.

This is why shart wujub (condition of obligation) and shart sihha (condition of validity) pull apart so sharply here. Disbelievers are obligated (mukhatabun) by the furu', a matter of wujub. They cannot discharge that obligation validly until Islam is in place, a matter of sihha. The obligation exists before the means to fulfill it validly does.

What Depends on Niyyah. Acts whose validity depends on an intention, such as salah, cannot be validly performed by a disbeliever, since the required niyyah is itself unavailable to someone outside Islam.

(1)

What Does Not Depend on Niyyah. Acts whose validity does not depend on an intention, such as avoiding what is prohibited, or permissible acts like freeing a slave or it'am al-miskin, feeding the poor, as an expiation (kaffarah), are valid from a disbeliever even before Islam. A related terminological note is worth flagging here: sadaqah in fiqh usage can mean either a purely voluntary charitable gift or, in other contexts, zakah itself, the obligatory charity. Which sense is meant has to be read from context, since the same word covers both.

(2)

Practically, this cashes out differently in this world versus the next. In dunya, disbelievers are not actually compelled to perform salah, pay zakah, or fast, since none of it could be validly discharged from them anyway before Islam, and the existence of ahl al-dhimmah, non-Muslims living permanently under Muslim rule, is itself the standing proof of this: dhimmis are not forced through the furu' the way Muslims are, because compulsion presupposes a validity that isn't there. In akhira, by contrast, they remain fully liable (mu'akhadh) for both usul and furu' together, per everything established above. The 'amm (general) address to mankind and the khass (specific) machinery of legal enforcement in this world are simply answering different questions, and this gap between them is exactly why the two can come apart the way they do.

Once a disbeliever embraces Islam, none of the furu' missed beforehand are held against them or need to be made up. The obligation existed the whole time, but the door to fulfilling it validly only opened with Islam, and the past is not revisited once it does.

Niyyah Is Not the Same as 'Azm

The niyyah that unlocks valid worship is a specific technical thing, and it is worth separating from a related but distinct idea, 'azm, mere resolve or determination.

Niyyah (Intention). In the Shafi'i school, niyyah must be coupled with the actual fi'l, the performance of the act itself, in order to count for validity in 'ibadat. Intending to pray tomorrow does not itself constitute the niyyah that makes tomorrow's prayer valid. The niyyah has to accompany the act at the moment it is performed.

(1)

'Azm (Resolve). A prior determination or firm intention to do something in the future, formed ahead of time and detached from the act itself. 'Azm on its own is not sufficient to satisfy the niyyah a valid 'ibadah requires, precisely because it is not tied to the moment of performance the way niyyah must be.

(2)

This distinction also connects to a genuine cross-madhhab difference on where niyyah fits structurally. In the Shafi'i school, niyyah is counted as a rukn, a pillar of the act itself. In the Hanafi school, it is counted as a shart, a condition for the act rather than one of its constituent pillars. The two schools agree niyyah is required. They disagree on what kind of requirement it is, a distinction that matters for exactly where in an act's structure it needs to occur.

Shahada, Ishhad, and Internal vs. External Ahkam

A related question is whether entering Islam has to be public to count. The answer separates two different levels entirely.

Internally, Before Allah. Sincere belief itself is what actually matters and is sufficient on its own. A person who believes sincerely in secret (sirran) has genuinely entered Islam before Allah, regardless of whether anyone else knows.

(1)

Externally, For Legal Purposes. Being recognized as Muslim within the community, for matters like inheritance or marriage, requires ishhad, open testimony before witnesses, to actually establish that status in a way the community and its courts can act on.

(2)

The internal ahkam and the external ahkam are answering two different questions, not competing over the same one. A loose assumption worth correcting directly here too: Islam as a legal status does not require having already fulfilled all of the arkan, the five pillars in full. Technically, the status of being Muslim begins the moment the shahada is spoken. Full practice of salah, zakah, and the rest follows from that status, rather than being the precondition for it.

The Convert Who Dies Moments Later

A concrete case pulls the whole discussion together: someone who speaks the shahada only just before dying. Are they answerable for the prayers they missed across their life as a disbeliever?

Only for prayers whose time entered after their conversion. This follows directly from everything above: taklif with respect to salah as something owed and makeable-up only attaches once Islam is in place, so a prayer whose window had already opened and closed before shahada was never validly performable by them and is not owed afterward either. The same logic that wipes clean a lifetime of missed furu' upon conversion applies just as much to someone whose "lifetime" as a Muslim lasted only minutes.

The Parallel With a Person in Hadath

The relationship between a disbeliever and prayer mirrors the relationship between someone in hadath, a state requiring purification, and prayer. In both cases it is impossible to command the person to perform a valid prayer while the underlying impediment, kufr or hadath, is still in place. What is actually being commanded is to remove the impediment first, Islam in the one case and purification in the other, and only then to pray. The address was never really "perform this prayer as you currently are." It was always "remove what stands in the way, then perform it."

Text
وَالنَّهْيُ: اسْتِدْعَاءُ التَّرْكِ بِالْقَوْلِ مِمَّنْ هُوَ دُونَهُ عَلَى سَبِيلِ الْوُجُوبِ، وَيَدُلُّ عَلَى فَسَادِ الْمَنْهِيِّ عَنْهُ.
Vocabulary
  • النَّهْيal-nahy, prohibition, the mirror image of al-amr
  • التَّرْكal-tark, the leaving off or non-performance of an act
  • فَسَادfasad, corruption or invalidity, here of the act being prohibited
Translation
Nahy is the seeking of abstention, by speech, from someone of lesser standing than the speaker, on the basis of obligation, and it indicates the corruption of the thing prohibited.
Commentary

Nahy mirrors the definition of amr given earlier almost word for word, with tark (leaving off an act) substituted for fi'l (performing an act). This parallel structure is deliberate: amr and nahy are being defined as structurally symmetrical categories, which is also why the "reciprocal" rule above is able to move so cleanly between them.

Nahy Indicates Corruption of the Act. Beyond simply obligating abstention, a prohibition also signals that the prohibited act itself is fasid, defective or invalid, when performed. This is why an act done in violation of a clear prohibition is not merely sinful but typically treated as legally void or unsound in its own right, rather than sinful yet still valid.
